--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (General aspects of nuclear physics)
 
 \seminormal

	- Before neutrons were found, it was thought that the atom consists only of protons and electrons, although the principles of holding the atom together couldn't be explained 
	
	- Finding neutron (theoretical prediction by Rutherford 1920, experimental discovery by Chadwick 1932) rapidly helped to build models for the overall composition of the atomic nucleus 
	
	- But even today the models describing the nucleus are not fully satisfactory

- Some nuclei are inert and stable, some are not, releasing enormous amounts of energy

- Nuclear physics has resulted in several applications:
-  \semismall
	-  Energy applications of nuclear reactions
- Radioactivity \& radioactive isotopes (radioactive dating, tracers, ...)
- Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) imaging (medical imaging)
- Neutron activation analysis (NAA) (concentrations of elements)

--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (General aspects of atomic nucleus)

	- Size of atom is $0.1–1 \mathrm{\;nm}$, while the size of nucleus is 1–10\;fm ($10^{-5}$ of the whole atom)

- Although electrons are bound to nuclei, their binding energies are negligible compared with the energies within the nucleus

- On the other hand, magnetic moment of nucleus $ (  \frac{e \hbar}{2 m_{p}} ) $ is small $\sim 1/2000$ compared with the magnetic moment of electrons (Bohr magneton $\frac{e \hbar}{2 m_{e}}$)

	- Notation for the isotope of an element, i.e. a nuclide, ${}_{Z}^{A}X$:
$$\small
{rl} 
  A&=  ~Atomic mass number$^*$ = integer) \approx \text {Atomic weight in amu} \\ 
A=Z+N \,,~where \quad  Z&=\text {~Atomic number (number of protons)}^\dagger \rightarrow \text {charge for the nucleus} \\ 
N&=\text {~Neutron number}

$$
- Mass of nucleus $\approx A \cdot m$, where $m \approx m_{p} \approx m_{n}$

\[
 1 \;u \equiv \frac{m_{C-12}}{12} = 1.660540 \times 10^{-27} \mathrm{\;kg}
\]

- Stable nuclei typically have about the same number of protons and neutrons: 
\[
 Z \approx N
 \]

 (Basic structure of atomic nucleus)

\semismall

	- There are more than 3000 known nuclides, of which 266 are known to be stable
- 
	 - The stable nuclei are naturally abundant
	- No isotopes absent from nature are stable

- The number of stable isotopes for each $Z$ vary, e.g.:
- 
	 - Helium has two naturally occurring isotopes: He-3 (99.99986%) and He-4 $(0.00014 %)$
- Carbon has two naturally occurring isotopes: C-12 (98.90%) and C-13 (1.10%)
- Aluminum has only one naturally abundant isotope Al-27
- Tin has 10: $\mathrm{Sn}-112 \rightarrow$ Sn-124 (except Sn-121, having half-life of 55 years)

- Unstable isotopes can be made artificially, but they vanish via radioactive decay

- Unstable isotopes can also be abundant in nature, if they are generated by a radioactive decay from another nuclei

- For example, uranium-238 and thorium-232 are not stable, but still they are more abundant in nature than silver

--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (Size of the nucleus)

	- Three experimental methods exist to determine the sizes of nuclei	
	- Each one gives slightly different results, but they all are within the same order of magnitude 
 \seminormal
	- Scattering of $\alpha$-particles
	 \small
		- Rutherford bombarded thin foil of gold with $\alpha$ particles $ (={ }^{4} \mathrm{He}$ nuclei)

- Scattering of electrons
	 \small
		- Hofstadter bombarded nuclei with electrons of $200-500 \mathrm{\;MeV}$

- Attenuation of a beam of fast neutrons
	 \small
		- Cross section of attenuation is obtained

{8.5cm}

	- All of these give the same type of result for the radius of roughly spherical nucleus:
\[
r  =  R_{0} A^{1 / 3}
\]
where $R_{0} \approx 1.2 \mathrm{\;fm}$ (1.1--1.4 fm is measured) 

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_3.png]

--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (Size of the nucleus)

- The above proportionality ($r \propto A^{1/3}$) implies the nuclear volume to be
\[
V_{t o t}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^{3}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi (  R_{0} A^{1 / 3} )  ^{3}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi R_{0}^{3} \times A
\]
- The neutrons and protons have nearly equal mass ($m_{p} \approx m_{n} \approx m_{nucleon}$), in which case the total mass of the nucleus is proportional to  $A$, implying that all nuclei have roughly the same density:
\[
\rho  = \fraca{\text {mass}}{\text {volume}}  = \fraca{A \times m_{\text {nucleon}}}{A \times \frac{4}{3} \pi R_{0}^{3}} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17} \mathrm{\;kg} / \mathrm{m}^{3}
\]

- Constant density suggests that nucleons behave as incompressible objects packed as close together as possible, i.e. nucleons have a strongly repulsive hard core

- Effective radius of an individual nucleon is approximately $ R_{0} \sim 1 \mathrm{\;fm}$

--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (Rutherford's model)

\semismall  

{8.5cm}

	- Rutherford bombarded $\mathrm{Pb}$ with $\alpha$ particles with increasing energy. At energies greater than $\approx 27 \mathrm{\;MeV}$, the incoming $\alpha$ particles started to interact with Pb-nucleus via attractive nuclear force

- Also, experiments on beta decay from mirror nuclides (isobars) $ (  A_{1}=A_{2} )$, but $Z_{1}=N_{2}$ and $( N_{1}=Z_{2} ) $, gave indication on the size of nucleus 
\implies{ Differences in the two nuclei, e.g. ${ }^{15} \mathrm{O}$ and ${ }^{15} \mathrm{N}$, are only of electrostatic origin }

- By integration of the Coulombic energy it is straightforward to show that the electrostatic energy of a sphere of charge $Q=Z e$ with radius $R$ is:

\[
U  = \frac{3}{5} \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{Q^{2}}{R}  = \frac{3}{5} \frac{k Q^{2}}{R} = \frac{3}{5} \frac{k Z^{2} e^{2}}{R}
\]

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_3.png]

--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (Rutherford's model)

	- Therefore, if radioactive ${ }^{15} \mathrm{O}$ decays into ${ }^{15} \mathrm{N}$ by emitting a $\beta$-particle (an electron), then the energy difference between ${ }^{15} \mathrm{O}$ and ${ }^{15} \mathrm{N}$ would be
\[
\Delta U  = \frac{3}{5} \frac{k e^{2}}{R} [Z^{2}-(Z-1)^{2} ]
\]
- with $Z=8$, from where the radius of nucleus $R$ can be estimated

- Example: Calculate the radius for the lightest and heaviest nuclei, i.e. for ${ }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}$ and ${ }^{238}_{92}U$. The radius is given by $r_{0}=1.2 \times A^{1/3} \mathrm{fm}$ so:
$$

 r_{He} & = 1.2 \times (4)^{1/3} \mathrm{\;fm}=1.90 \mathrm{\;fm} \\
r_{U} & = 1.2 \times (238)^{1/3} \mathrm{\;fm}=7.42 \mathrm{\;fm}

$$
 \implies{ The lightest and heaviest nuclei are only a factor 4 different \\
 		in physical size }

\semismall

{7.5cm}

	- The scattering of high-energy electrons from nuclei show consistent dependency on the atomic number $Z$

- It is assumed that a 'skin' exists on the surface, defined to lie between $10 %$ and $90 %$ of the central core density of the nucleus, and $r_{0}$ being at the $50 %$ value of the charge density

- A good approximation for radial profile of nucleon density is: 

\[
\hspace{3cm} \rho(r)=\fraca{\rho_{n}}{1+e^{ (  r-r_{0} )   / a}}
\]
where:
$$

&\rho_{n}=0.17 \;\frac{\text {nucleon}}{\;fm^{3}} \\
&r_{0}= (  1.18 A^{1/3}-0.48 )   \;fm \\
&a=0.55 \mathrm{\;fm} 

$$

--- slide: Basic Structure ---

 (Short summary)

\seminormal

	- Facts:
- 
	 - Nucleon density of the nucleus is ${\sim}$constant, independent of element - Charge density in the nucleus is ${\sim}$constant, independent of element

- Conclusions:
If density is constant, adding a nucleon $(A \rightarrow A+1)$ does not increase density. So, if there is an attractive force it is definitely not like electrical force, which would be increasing by a factor of $A$ if one charge is added. So, the nuclear forces need to have a short range, ca. size of one nucleon

- The nuclear forces are very strong, compared to electrical forces (it takes $13.6 \mathrm{\;eV}$ to remove the electron from hydrogen, but $8 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ to remove a nucleon from the nucleus)

- The attractive interaction energy is approximately the same for protons and neutrons. Thus, nuclear forces are independent of electrical charge

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Binding forces)

	- We call the force between the nucleons the strong force. It is one of the four fundamental forces:

	- Strong $(\leftrightarrow$ internucleon attraction)
- Electromagnetic
- Weak
- Gravitational

- Strong force reaches only to roughly 1-2 fm, then, for larger distances it is practically zero. This fact leads to stability of the nucleus

{8.25cm}

	- For larger distances, electromagnetic forces are significant
	\implies{ Heavier elements, that are larger in size than ${\sim}$2~fm, are affected by the electrostatic forces (repulsion between protons) }

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_Table_2.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Binding forces)

\seminormal

	- Figure 4 gives an outline of the strong force:
 \small
	- Short range attraction
- Strong repulsion for hard core
- Independent on whether we deal with proton or neutron
- Depends on the orientation of nuclear spins
- No formula yet exists for the strong force

{8.7cm}

	- Combined with a Coulombic repulsive force between protons, the strong force potential well of Figure 4 is modified to have a repulsive barrier for the larger radii

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_1_cropped.png]

- From the model presented, one might expect that we could combine any number of protons and neutrons together and form stable nuclei. However only some combinations form a stable nucleus while others are unstable

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_4.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Theoretical model for the stability)

	- Two-Nucleon Nuclei\\
	Consider two nucleons bound together by strong force, in a narrow deep potential well shown previously

- 
	 - Two protons (p-p): Internucleon attraction plus Coulombic repulsion, probably the most unstable of these three

- Two neutrons $(\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{n})$: Only internucleon attraction 
- Proton-neutron pair (p-n): Only internucleon attraction $\rightarrow$ deuteron

- It turns out, that only p-n combination of these three forms a bound nucleus. Why? The internucleon attraction is spin-dependent, being stronger if the spins are aligned. Secondly, exclusion principle kicks in, which is crucial for all nuclei. Protons and neutrons are spin-1/2 fermions, which cannot occupy the same state in a same system

--- slide: Binding ---

	- The lowest energy possible for two nucleons is a ground state with spins aligned (orientation of stronger attraction). Such a state is forbidden for $\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{n}$ and $\mathrm{p}-\mathrm{p}$ systems due to the exclusion principle
	
	- It turns out that deuteron is only barely bound and does not have any excited bound states

{8cm}

	- And if deuteron cannot find any other way to bind, then the other two pairs should not bind at all

- In Figure 5 the energy in a deuteron is depicted.

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_5.png]

 Two nucleons occupy the lone bound state in a potential well arising from their mutual attraction

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Strong internucleon attraction)

\semismall

	-  \semismall
	 - A two-nucleon has one bond, i.e, half a bond per nucleon 
	 - A three nucleon has three, i.e. one bond per nucleon 
	 - A four-nucleon has six, i.e. $1.5$ bonds per nucleon 
	 \implies{number of bonds increases}

{7.4cm}

	- This increasing trend continues only until the nucleons are packed tightly together and the nearest neighbours surround each other. Then each surrounded nucleon will have the same maximum number of bonds

- This ratio is essentially area over volume $=\frac{4 \pi r^{2}}{4 / 3 \pi r^{3}} \propto \frac{1}{r}$ 
- The average number of bonds per nucleon approach a constant-valued  binding energy per nucleon (not bonds per nucleon), i.e, the energy required to pull all nucleons apart

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_6.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Coulomb repulsion)

{9.0cm}

	- All pairs of protons repel each other, which shifts the proton energies slightly up, closer to the top of the finite well
	
- In small nuclei, all nucleons touch, and there is a net attraction between them. But there are only few bonds per nucleon

- In larger nuclei, pairs of protons shift apart so that the strong force cannot affect anymore \implies{ These pairs add a net repulsion }

- Combining these two effects, we expect a maximum in the binding energy per nucleon

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_8.png]

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_7.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

 (The exclusion principle)

	- According to exclusion principle the lowest energy would correspond to a case with equal number of protons and neutrons
	
	- In Figure 9, we have initially equal number of protons and neutrons. Keeping $A$ fixed, we change one proton to neutron. Because lower energy levels are filled, we need to place the neutron at higher state,

{6.5cm}

- i.e. total energy will increase 

- The same argument applies, if we change a neutron to a proton
	- Thus the state $Z=N$ is of the lowest energy

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_9.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

\seminormal

{8.75cm}

	- Now we take into account the Coulomb repulsion between the protons. Consider a small nuclei where all nucleons touch, all proton repulsions are overwhelmed by the strong attraction, so $Z \cong N$ should be more stable

- In a large nuclei, uncompensated Coulomb repulsions shift the proton states upwards. Thus is could be expected that at lowest energy state, the tops of neutron and proton levels should be equal, thus this case should have $N>Z$

- This reasoning predicts that the nucleons should be most tightly bound in a nuclei with intermediate mass number. The binding energy per nucleon should have a relative maximum at $Z \cong N$ for small nuclei and at $N>Z$ for larger nuclei

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_10.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Experimental facts regarding stability)

	- The validity for our model on the nuclear forces comes from the sums of the experimentally extracted nuclear masses

- If we need to use energy to pull separate nucleons out of the nuclei, it must be that the mass of the nuclei is less than the sum of the masses of the parts

- Clearly, e.g. deuteron weighs less than the sum of its parts:

{8cm}

- 
	 - Deuteron mass $m_D = 2.013553 \mathrm{\;u}$ 
	- ~
	$$
	m_p  +  m_n &= 1.007276 \mathrm{\;u}+1.008665 \mathrm{\;u} \\&=2.015941 \mathrm{\;u}
	$$
	 
- Mass of parts $-$ mass of whole 
$$
=2.015941 \;u   - 2.013553 \;u  =  + 0.002388 \;u
$$

- \implies{ We need to do work to pull the deuteron apart }

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_11.png]

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Binding energy of the nucleus)

	- We define binding energy $B E$ for an arbitrary nucleus, as the energy to pull all of the nucleons apart:
\[
BE  = m_{f} c^{2}  - m_{i} c^{2}  =   ( mass of parts  - \text {mass of whole} )\, c^{2}
\]

- For deuteron:
$$

B E &  =  (  0.002388 \;u \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27} \mathrm{\;kg} )   (  3 \times 10^{8} \frac{\mathrm{m}}{s} )^{2} \\
&  = 3.57 \times 10^{-13} \mathrm{\;J}  = 2.22 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$
Also e.g. for hydrogen atom, one needs $13.6 \mathrm{\;eV}$ to pull the electron apart. The atom weighs $2.4 \times 10^{-35} \mathrm{\;kg}$ less than its parts separately

- Note that the fractional mass change $\Delta m$ of hydrogen atom is $2.4 \times 10^{-35} \mathrm{\;kg} / 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \mathrm{\;kg} \cong 10^{-8}$ 
- But $\Delta m$ for deuteron is $0.002388 \mathrm{\;u} / 2.013553 \mathrm{\;u} \cong 10^{-3}$, a large value!

--- slide: Binding ---

 (Binding energy of the nucleus)

	- The theoretical model gives us the binding energy per nucleon, for e.g. deuteron this would be: $2.22 \mathrm{\;MeV} / 2=1.11 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ per nucleon. In general:
\[
BE  =  ( Z m_{H}  + N m_{n}  - M_{{}^{A}_{Z}X} ) c^{2} \,, \qquad M_{{}^{A}_{Z}X} \equiv m_a
\]

- The formula contains the mass of hydrogen atom $m_H$ ($m_p+m_e$), 

{6.5cm}

	- which cancels the electrons' masses in the atomic mass term $M_{{}^{A}_{Z}X}$, that also contains the electron masses
	
	- Still, this is an approximation, since e.g.~electron binding energies are not taken into account (being rather small)

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Shape of the nucleus)

	- Most of the atomic nuclei are nearly spherical
	- Mostly only rare earth elements $(Z=57-71)$ are ellipsoidal, with the major axis differing from the minor axis about 20 percent

- In those heavy nuclei, their inner atomic electron wave functions even slightly penetrate the nucleus. Deviations from the spherical shape, which correspond to deviations in the nuclear charge distributions, show up as small changes in the atomic energy levels

- The shape of the nucleus is shown in the average value of the electric quadrupole moment:
\[
< Q >  = Z \int \psi^{*} [ 3 (z_{a v}^{2} )_{a v}  -  (x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2} )_{a v}] \psi  \, d V
\]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Shape of the nucleus)

{10.0cm}

	- So, if the quadrupole moment is positive, the shape is prolate ellipsoid, i.e. like a 'vertical watermelon' 
	- If the moment is zero, the shape is a sphere 

{6.5cm}

	- If the quadrupole moment is negative, then the shape is an oblate ellipsoid, i.e. more like a 'flattened pumpkin'

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_10.png]

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_11_cropped.png]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Nuclear models)

	- There are several theoretical models to describe the atomic nucleus. Two most useful ones are:
- 
	- Liquid drop model

	- Assumes nucleus as a round droplet of incompressible liquid
- Allows to predict the binding energy of the nucleus

- Shell model
- 
	- Adapts the models for electrons as oscillators in a deep quantum well
- Predicts e.g. the most stable nuclei, based on so-called magic numbers

- Each model has its emphasis. Although they agree in some ways, they are still only approximate, relying on unique assumptions and simplifications. To understand the atomic nucleus, there is still room for complementary models

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (The liquid drop model)

	- Since the density of the nucleus was seen to be radial rather homogeneous, and also since all nuclei seem to have nearly the same density, one natural way to describe the nucleus is the liquid drop model

- Analogy with a droplet of liquid works fairly well on the atomic nuclei: 
- 
	- Liquid consists of relatively closely packed, disorganised molecules, which attract each other for larger separations 
	- At small separations become strongly repulsive, leading to incompressibility, such as in the case of water

- To move a water molecule to the surface of a droplet, we need to pull it away from those molecules, that would otherwise attract it from the sides. This requires energy, and the lowest energy state, to minimize the surface, will then be a sphere

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (The liquid drop model)

\semismall

	- In liquid the interior particles are attracted to all particles in the immediate vicinity

- If all the particles would be inside the droplet, then the binding energy would be directly proportional to the number of particles.

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop.pdf]

 However, the particles on the surface have fewer neighbours, i.e. fewer contacts, or bonds, with others \implies{ Reduction of the binding energy }

{8.5cm}

	- Idea is to build a general formula for the binding energy for a droplet of nucleons

- Altogether five contributions (i.e. five terms) affect the total binding energy of the nucleus

- We will define $c_{1}, c_{2}, c_{3}, c_{4}$, and $c_{5}$ by fitting experimental data to the formed model [which must be $f(A,N,Z)$]

- Our aim is to build a semi-empirical formula for the binding energy of known nuclei

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Volume term)

	- Particles totally inside the droplet, are fully surrounded by neighbouring particles. If all the particles in the droplet would be in the interior, then the binding energy would be directly proportional to the number of particles in the droplet, i.e. nucleons in the nucleus.
\[
BE \propto c_{1} A
\]

{8.5cm}

- The proportionality constant $c_{1}$ can be theoretically estimated, but here it is more convenient to first build the whole formula and then fit the constants to empirical data

	- So, volume term is given by  \[
c_{1} A
\]

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop_interior.pdf]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Volume term)

\semismall

	- Q: Is fitting constants $c_{i}$ to empirical data really a theory/useful theory?
- A: It is a model of a very complex system, guided by experimental information

- The known binding energies per nucleon describe a certain shape of a function of $\mathrm{N}$ and $\mathrm{Z}$, for which the mathematical form cannot be explicitly seen

	- With a simple polynomial fit on $Z$ and $N$, such as e.g.:
\[
c_{1} Z^{2}  + c_{2} N  + c_{3}
\]

{6.5cm}

- it would be impossible fo fulfil the task, and also, not much of physical insight would be obtained 

- The fact that the liquid drop model fits so well to the data, gives strong evidence that the made assumptions are valid 

- It can also be used for further predictions of yet unknown nuclides

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_14.png]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Surface term)

	- For surface nucleons, there are fewer neighbours attracting them than in the interior. So, they should be easier to extract \implies{ Binding energy of surface nucleons is lower than for the interior nucleons }

- Recalling
$ \quad 
\text {Volume}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^{3} \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = ( \frac{3}{4 \pi} \text {Volume} )^{1 / 3}
$
- The surface area becomes $=4 \pi r^{2} =4 \pi ( \frac{3}{4 \pi} Volume)^{2 / 3} \propto$ Volume$^{2 / 3}$

{8.5cm}

- The total binding energy should be reduced by a factor proportional to the surface area, which is proportional to Volume$^{2 / 3}$

	- And, we know that volume is proportional to the number of nucleons, resulting in:
\[
BE \propto  - c_{2} A^{2 / 3}
\]

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop_surface.pdf]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Coulomb term)

	- Coulomb interaction between the protons raises the overall potential energy $\Rightarrow$ Coulomb interaction reduces the binding energy 

- There are $Z$ protons in the nucleus, which each interact (repel) with the $Z-1$ other protons

- Therefore, the Coulombic energy term, proportional to $\frac{e^{2}}{r}$, will contribute a term proportional to:

{8.5cm}

	- 
	$$
	
\frac{Z(Z-1)}{\text {average proton separation}} &\propto \frac{Z(Z-1)}{droplet radius}\\ 
&\propto \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1 / 3}}

$$
-  $$ \Rightarrow \qquad 
B E \propto -c_{3} \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1 / 3}} \hspace{4cm}
$$

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop_coulomb.pdf]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Asymmetry term)

{8cm}

	- For smaller nuclei $N \cong Z$ but for larger nuclei $N>Z$. This has an effect on the energy level occupation balance between neutrons and protons. The effect is quantum mechanical, and occurs due to the exclusion principle

- If there is an excess of neutrons, for example, then a larger number of energy levels gets filled up (two neutrons per level) and the energy of the system increases

- And, because we have a process that increases the energy, then it will decrease the binding energy

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_13.png]

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Asymmetry term)

\small

	- Examine now a situation where the neutrons and protons have filled the energy levels at equal heights, $E_{F}$, and assume also that the energy levels are equally spaced, $\delta E$ apart

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_15.png]

{8.75cm}

	- If $j$ neutrons are now changed to protons (or vice versa), the top neutrons must go from neutron level $E_{F}$ to proton level $E_{F}+(j / 2) \delta E$. The others must make an equal jump. If the energy of each particle increases by $(j / 2) \delta E$ the total energy increases by $(j^{2} / 2) \delta E$

- Now, let's express $j$ in terms of $N$ and $Z$. First $N=Z$, and gain $j$ protons and lose $j$ neutrons. $Z-N$ must now be $2 j$, giving $j=\frac{1}{2}(Z-N)$. Thus, an asymmetry between $Z$ and $N$ should decrease the binding energy (increase the total energy), by a factor of
\[
(N-Z)^{2} \delta E  = (A-2 Z)^{2} \delta E \quad \Rightarrow \quad BE \propto -c_{4} \frac{(A-2 Z)^{2}}{A}
\]
where division by $A$ normalizes the term

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Pairing term)

	- As known from the data, nuclear force favours pairing of protons and of neutrons. This last term$^{*}$ gives a better fit to the data

- The term takes into account the fact that the nucleon–nucleon interaction does not merely give rise to an overall average potential for the nucleons, but has also some special characteristics similar to the pairing interaction between two electrons in the superconductor forming a Cooper pair

- This energy term is:
- 
	- positive (more binding) if both $Z$ and $N$ are even,
- negative (less binding) if both $Z$ and $N$ are odd, zero otherwise

\footnoteleft{{$^*$Note, this  5$^{\text {th}}$ term (pairing term) is not included by the textbook by Harris}}

--- slide: Nuclear Models ---

 (Semi-empirical binding energy formula)

	- Summing up the results, we have a semi-empirical formula for the binding energy of the atomic nucleus, sometimes also called Weizsäcker's formula:
	\[
B E = c_{1} A  - c_{2} A^{2 / 3}  - c_{3} \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1 / 3}}  - c_{4} \frac{(A-2 Z)^{2}}{A} \pm c_{5} A^{-4 / 3}
\]
- where 
$$

&c_{1}=15.8 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
&c_{2}=17.8 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
&c_{3}=0.71 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
&c_{4}=23.7 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
&c_{5}=15.8 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$

 (Radioactive Decay Law)

	- A radioactive decay for each nucleus can happen only once. The process is of statistical nature, and for an individual nucleus, only an estimated average time can be given for the decay

- If the amount of nuclei $N$ is large enough, the statistical nature of the decay process will result in a distinct law of decay 

- The number of decayed nuclei, i.e. change in the number of nuclei, $d N$, will be proportional to both time and number of radioactive nuclei:
\[
\{}\! {l}
d N \propto d t \\
d N \propto N
 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad d N \propto N d t
\]
and 
\[
\Rightarrow \qquad \frac{d N}{d t} \propto N \quad \text { i.e. } \quad \frac{d N}{d t} =  - \lambda N \quad \text { or } \quad \frac{d N}{N}  =  - \lambda d t
\]

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Radioactive Decay Law)

	- We can solve the differential equation and thereby obtain time dependence of the number of remaining radioactive nuclei:
\[
\frac{d N}{N}  =  - \lambda d t \quad \Rightarrow \quad \int_{N_{0}}^{N} \frac{d N^{\prime}}{N^{\prime}}  =  - \lambda \int_{0}^{t} d t \quad \Rightarrow \quad \ln \frac{N}{N_{0}} =  - \lambda t \quad \Rightarrow \quad N(t)= N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}
\]
- where decay constant $\lambda$ ($[\lambda]=\frac{1}{s}$) is characteristic to each radioactive nuclei

- Define a positive quantity $R=\lambda N(t)  =  |\! \frac{d N}{dt} \!| $,  known as the decay rate

- We can also write the decay rate as: 
\[
 R(t)= | \frac{d N}{d t} |  = \lambda N(t) = \lambda N_{0} e^{-\lambda t} = R_{0} e^{-\lambda t}
\]

- Note, that both the number of nuclei $N$ and the decay rate $R$ decrease exponentially with time

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Radioactive Decay Law)

\seminormal

	- The time when half of the original nuclei are left is called half-life $T_{1 / 2}$:

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_26.png]

\[
 N ( T_{1 / 2} )   =  N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1 / 2}}  = \frac{1}{2} N_{0} \qquad
\Rightarrow \qquad  - \lambda T_{1 / 2} = \ln \frac{1}{2} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1 / 2}}
\]
- Nuclear binding processes are complex, and often several alternative decay channels exist. Therefore it is difficult to predict $T_{1 / 2}$ of a nuclide. Data for most of the isotopes exists, and $T_{1 / 2}$ varies from $10^{-22} s$ to $10^{17}$ years

{6.5cm}

- For example the half-life of carbon-14, an isotope used in carbon dating, is 5730 years. For americium-241, used in ordinary household smoke alarms, $T_{1 / 2}=432.2$ years

- An example of a somewhat short-lived isotope is fluorine-18, an isotope commonly used in nuclear medicine, has a half-life of 109 min  

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Radioactive Decay Law)

	- The decay rate, $R(t)=\lambda N$, is also used to indicate the activity (or 'radioactivity') of the given amount of radioactive nuclei 
\[
[ R ]  =  number of radioactive nuclei decaying per unit time
\]

- The SI-unit of radioactivity is becquerel (Bq), while the old unit (still widely used) is curie (Ci)
\[
1 \mathrm{\;Bq}  = 1 \text {\;decay} / \mathrm{s} \quad , \quad 
1 \mathrm{\;Ci}  =  3.7 \times 10^{10} \text {\;decay} / \mathrm{s}=3.7 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{\;Bq}
\]
- $1 \mathrm{\;Ci}$ equals activity of a $1 \mathrm{\;g}$ of radium $({ }_{~88}^{226}Ra$,  $T_{1/2}=1600$ years)

- A radiation source with activity of $1 \mathrm{\;mCi}=37 \mathrm{\;MBq}$ may be considered a significantly strong source (slightly depending on the kind of radiation emitted)

- The activity of ${ }^{241} \mathrm{Am}$ in a household smoke alarm is typically $1 \;\mu \mathrm{Ci}=37 \mathrm{\;kBq}$

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Example calculation)

	- Q: A vessel holds $2 \;\mu$g of tritium (half-life $T_{1/2}=12.3\;a$, atomic mass 3.02\;u).
(a) What are its initial decay rate and activity?
(b) How much time will elapse before the decay rate falls to $1 %$ of its initital value?

- A: (a) Decay rate is $R(t)=\lambda N$, so the initial decay rate will be:
\[
R_{0} = R(t=0) = \lambda N_{0} = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1 / 2}} \frac{m_{tot}}{m_{1}}  = \frac{\ln 2}{12.3 \;y} \frac{2 \times 10^{-9} \;kg}{3.02 \;u \cdot 1.66 \times 10^{-27} \;kg / u}  = 7.1 \times 10^{8} \;s^{-1}
\]
And, the corresponding activity will be $7.1 \times 10^{8} \mathrm{\;Bq}=710 \mathrm{\;MBq}$

- (b) We ask, at which time $\lambda N(t)=\frac{1}{100} \lambda N_{0}$? 
$$

 &\Rightarrow \qquad& \lambda N_{0} e^{-\lambda t} & = \frac{1}{100} \lambda N_{0} 
\qquad \Rightarrow \qquad  - \lambda t = \ln \frac{1}{100} \\
 &\Rightarrow \qquad&  t& =  - \ln \frac{1}{100} / ( \frac{\ln 2}{12,3 \;a} \,) = 2,6 \times 10^{9} \;s=82 \;a

$$

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Chain of radioactive decays)

	- Consider a chain of radioactive decays: $A \stackrel{\lambda_{A}}{\rightarrow} B \stackrel{\lambda_{B}}{\rightarrow} C$, decay constants for $A$ and $B$ being $\lambda_{A}$ and $\lambda_{B}$, respectively 
	- Our goal is to find the variation of nuclide $B$ as a function of time, $N_{B}=N_{B}(t)$, assuming that the initial numbers (at $t=0)$ of the radioactive nuclides $A$ and $B$ would be $N_{A0}$ and $N_{B0}$, respectively

- Substance A decays at rate $\frac{d N_{A}}{d t} =-\lambda_{A} N_{A}$, resulting in $N_{A}(t)=N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}$

- Substance $B$ is formed by decay of A, and also decays itself:
\[
\frac{d N_{B}}{d t}  =  + \lambda_{A} N_{A} - \lambda_{B} N_{B} =  + \lambda_{A} N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} - \lambda_{B} N_{B}
\]

- This is a differential equation for $N_{B}$. To solve $N_{B}=N_{B}(t)$, first we make an educated guess with a trial function of the form: 
\[
N_{B}(t)  = N_{A 0} (c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} )
\]

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Chain of radioactive decays)

	- Plug in the trial form for $N_B$ and differentiate:
$$

& & \frac{d N_{B}}{d t} = N_{A 0} (-\lambda_{A} c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}  - \lambda_{B} c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} ) & =  + \lambda_{A} N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} - \lambda_{B} N_{A 0} (c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} ) \qquad \\
&\Rightarrow &  - \lambda_{A} c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}-\lambda_{B} c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t}& =  + \lambda_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} - \lambda_{B} (c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}+c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} ) \\
&\Rightarrow& (\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{B} c_{A}+\lambda_{A} c_{A} ) e^{-\lambda_{A} t}& = 0 \\
&\Rightarrow& \lambda_{A}-\lambda_{B} c_{A}+\lambda_{A} c_{A}&=0 \\
&\Rightarrow& c_{A}& = \fraca{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}

$$
Therefore, we obtain: 
\[
N_{B}(t)  = N_{A 0} ( \frac{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} )
\]
- where the factor $c_{B}$ is still unknown

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Chain of radioactive decays)

	- If the initial value for $N_{B}$ would be 0 at $t=0$, i.e.
\[
N_{B}(t=0)  = N_{A 0} ( \frac{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}} e^{-\lambda_{A} \cdot 0}  + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} \cdot 0} ) =0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad c_{B}=\frac{-\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}
\]

- Thus, we get the number of B nuclei $N_{B}(t)$:
\[
N_{B}(t)  = N_{A 0} \frac{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}
( e^{-\lambda_{A} t}-e^{-\lambda_{B} t} \,)
\]

- In practice, activity is an often-used quantity

- In this case, the activities for nuclei $A$ and $B$ are:
$$

R_A = \lambda_{A} N_{A} & = \lambda_{A} N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} \\
R_B = \lambda_{B} N_{B} & = N_{A 0} \frac{\lambda_{A} \lambda_{B}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}} ( e^{-\lambda_{A} t}-e^{-\lambda_{B} t} \,) 

$$

 (Generation of radioactive nuclides)

	- Radioactive nuclei are produced as a result of neutron capture by the nuclei (occasionally also electron and proton beams are used) 
	
	- For instance ${ }^{59} \mathrm{Co}$ is bombarded with neutrons to generate ${ }^{60} \mathrm{Co}$ which is radioactive with a half-life of $5.27$ years

- We assume that the radioactive nuclei is generated at constant rate $g$ nuclei per second, and a decay constant of $\lambda$, and calculate the number of radioactive nuclei produced per unit time:
$$

\frac{d N}{d t} & = g-\lambda N \\
\int_{N_{0}}^{N}  \frac{d N}{N-g / \lambda} & =  - \lambda \int_{0}^{t} d t \quad \Rightarrow  \quad \ln \fraca{N-g / \lambda}{N_{0}-g / \lambda}  =  - \lambda t

$$

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Generation of radioactive nuclides)

	- $$

& & \ln \frac{N-g / \lambda}{N_{0}-g / \lambda} & =  - \lambda t \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad N-g / \lambda& =  ( N_{0}-g / \lambda )  e^{-\lambda t} \\
&\Rightarrow \quad& N(t)& = \frac{g}{\lambda} ( 1-e^{-\lambda N} ) +N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}

$$

- If we start with zero nuclides, i.e. $N_{0}=0$, then the number of radioactive nuclei will be:
\[
N(t)  = \frac{g}{\lambda} ( 1-e^{-\lambda t} ) 
\]

- Note, that the maximum number of produced nuclei is $N_{\max }=\frac{g}{\lambda}$

- Furthermore, the activity of the sample is $\lambda N(t)=g ( 1-e^{-\lambda t} ) $, and its maximum value is $g$. Note, this example applies directly to neutron activation analysis (see later)

 (Cross section)

	- Consider a constant flux of small projectile particles towards a slab of target particles. Let's make some simplifying assumptions:

	- Projectile particles are small, point-like
- Target particles have a cross sectional area of $\sigma,~[\sigma]=m^{2}$
- Target particles have a low number density so that they do not cover or shadow each other

- Total (projected) area of target particles in the slab is:
\[
A^{\prime}  = \Underlinecomment{red}{n \cdot(A d x)}{\qquad Number of target particles within volume Adx} \cdot \sigma
\]

- Fractional coverage (projected area) of target particles
\[
\fraca{A^{\prime}}{A}  = \frac{n A d x \sigma}{A} = n \sigma d x
\]

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Exponential attenuation)

\seminormal

	- If each projectile interacts only once, the fraction of the projectile particles (of the incoming $N$ particles) which will be colliding within the slab thickness $d x$, will be:
\[
\frac{d N}{N}  = n \sigma d x
\]
- but it's more practical to consider the collided particles as being removed from the beam:
\[
\frac{d N}{N}  =  - n \sigma d x
\]

- Now, consider number of particles $N$ incoming into a slab of thickness $x$. Thus, we obtain the number of projectile particles which will remain in the beam, as a function of distance:
\[
\int_{N_{0}}^{N} \fraca{d N}{N} = \int_{0}^{x} - n \sigma d x \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad N(x) = N_{0} e^{-n \sigma x}
\]
Thus, we have obtained the law of exponential attenuation

--- slide: The Radioactive Decay Law ---

 (Cross section)

	- In the above, the projectile particles were for simplicity assumed to be point-like:
\[
\sigma  = \pi r^{2}
\]

- If both the projectile and the target have non-zero radii, then:
\[
\sigma  = \pi ( r_{1}  + r_{2} )^{2}
\]

- Quite often the actual effective cross sectional area is a function of collision energy

- In this case, the cross section of the interaction may differ markedly from the geometrical cross section of the target particle, and/or of the projectile

 (Cross section)

\seminormal

	- The customary unit for nuclear cross section is barn, where:
\[
1 \text { barn}  = 1 \mathrm{~b}  = 10^{-28} \mathrm{~m}^{2} = 100 \mathrm{\;fm}^{2}
\]
- Barn is not an SI unit, but it is convenient because its order of magnitude is the same as the geometrical cross section of atomic nucleus (the name barn comes from a common target) 

- Cross sections of most nuclear reactions depend on energy of the incident particle

Although ${ }_{\;48}^{113} C d$ isotopes constitute only $12 %$ of natural cadmium, this neutron capture cross section is so large, that cadmium is widely used in control rods for nuclear reactors

 (Cross section)

{7.0cm}

\]

 (Spontaneous and induced nuclear reactions)

	- Nuclear reactions can be:

	- Spontaneous: such as in the case of radioactivity, as described above. Then the $Q$-value of the reaction needs to be positive for the reaction to happen
- Induced: where we have an incoming (high-energy) particle to collide on the target nucleus and to enable the reaction

- When the target nucleus is bombarded with a projectile particle, the collision may be:

	- Elastic: Elastic scattering is reflection from the edge of the potential well of target nucleus $\rightarrow$ the incoming particle practically changes momentum and direction
- Inelastic: where a direct interaction occurs, and as a result, usually an intermediate 'compound nucleus' is formed first, before finally decaying into reaction products

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Inelastic scattering \& Induced nuclear reaction)

	- Inelastic scattering often occurs at intermediate energy levels, still less than ${\sim}140 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ 
	\implies{ no extra particles will yet form (such as mesons)}
	
	 - The collision of the target nucleus ($X$) with a projectile ($x$) creates a compound nucleus
	 
	 -  The compound nucleus decays into product nucleus ($Y$), often accompanied with an ejected particle ($y$) or particles
	 
	 - The general reaction scheme is either written as
\[
 x ~+~ X \quad \rightarrow \quad Y ~+~ y ~+~ Q  
\]
or slightly more concisely using parentheses convention:
\[
 target nucleus \;(\;  projectile \;,\; ejected particle \;)\; product nucleus
\]
i.e. as 
\[
 X(x, y) Y
 \]

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Collisions and Q-value of the reaction)

	- Whatever the nuclear reaction will be, it needs to conserve energy. For the nuclear reaction $X(x, y) Y$, the energy released in the reaction is called the $Q$-value:
\[
x ~+~ X \quad \rightarrow \quad Y ~+~ y ~+~ Q  \;,  where  \qquad Q=(m_{x}+m_{X}-m_{y}-m_{Y}) c^{2}
\]
- $Q>0$: exothermic reaction (initial particles are heavier than products) 
- $Q<0$: endothermic reaction (initial particles are lighter)

- Examples: 
$$

 \mathrm{n} ~+~ { }^{1} \mathrm{H} \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }^{2} \mathrm{H} ~+~ \gamma ~+~ 2.22 \mathrm{\;MeV} \quad  (Exothermic) \\
\gamma ~+~ { }^{2} \mathrm{H} \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }^{1} \mathrm{H} ~+~ \mathrm{n} ~-~ 2.22 \mathrm{\;MeV} \quad (Endothermic)

$$

- Endothermic reaction requires a threshold energy to be supplied 

- According to the above, the threshold energy is $|Q|$. But this is valid only for the center-of-mass (CM) coordinate frame

 (Kinetics)

\seminormal

{9.2cm}

	- We examine first the reaction in the CM frame with non-relativistic case of $x$ of mass $m$ (velocity $v_p$) incident on $X$ of mass $M$ (velocity $v_c$):

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_50_cropped_a]

$$

\vec{p}_{m}+\vec{p}_{M}=0 \qquad &\Rightarrow \qquad |\vec{p}_{m} |= |\vec{p}_{M} | \equiv p \\
  \qquad &\Rightarrow \qquad m v_p=M V_c \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{m}{M}=\frac{V_c}{v_p}  \\
  E_{CM}  = \frac{p^{2}}{2 m}&  + \frac{p^{2}}{2 M} = \frac{p^{2}}{2}  ( \frac{m+M}{m M} \,)  \,, \qquad CM frame

$$

- While on the laboratory frame,

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_50_cropped_b]

 where the velocity of the incident particle $\mathrm{x}$ is $v_p+V_c$, towards the target $M$, which is at rest, we obtain the momentum:
\[
p_{lab}  = m(v_p+V_c)  = m v_p ( 1+\frac{V_c}{v_p} \,)  = m v_p ( 1+\frac{m}{M} \,)  =  p ( 1+\frac{m}{M} \,) \hspace{3.5cm}
\] 
and for energy
\[
E_{lab}  = \frac{p_{lab}^{2}}{2 m}  = \frac{p^{2}}{2 m} ( \frac{m+M}{M} \,)^{2}  = \frac{m+M}{M} E_{C M} \hspace{3.5cm}
\]

 (Threshold energy and Example)

\seminormal

	- Thus, we get the threshold energy for the endothermic reaction in lab frame:

\]

	\[
	\quad { }_{\;6}^{13} C  ~+~  p \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{\;7}^{13}N  ~+~ n  \qquad \quad i.e. \qquad \quad { }_{\;6}^{13} C(p , n) { }_{\;7}^{13}N
	\]

Q&= ( m_{\text {initial }}-m_{final} \,)  c^{2} \\
&=(13.003355 \;u+1.007825 \;u-13.005738 \;u-1.008665 \;u) \times 931.5 \mathrm{\;MeV} / u \\
&=-3.00 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
E_{threshold}&=\frac{m+M}{M} |Q|  =  \frac{1.007825 \;u+13.003355 \;u}{13.003355 \;u} \times 3.00 \mathrm{\;MeV}  = 3.23 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$

 (Intermediate Nucleus ('Compound Nucleus'))

\semismall

{10.5cm}

	- Many of the low-energy nuclear reactions proceed as two-stage processes 
	- The projectile and target nuclide first form a temporary 'compound nucleus' which will be de-excited via an emission of one or more particles

 (Chain reaction)

\seminormal

	- Assume that each reaction requires a neutron to initiate the reaction, and that each reaction liberates $n$ product neutrons  
	\implies{ Each reaction will/can initiate $n$ other reactions }

$ \qquad

$

- But not all neutrons initiate further reactions (losses etc.), in practice it is better to replace the equation above by
\[
E_{j}  = E_{0} k^{j} \,, 
\]
if $k>1$ we will have a chain reaction, if $k<1$, the reaction will die out

	- Hydrogen is by far the most abundant element in the stars, and a series of fusion reactions known as the proton–proton cycle "burns" hydrogen towards helium-4
$$

& Step 1:\quad & { }_{1}^{1} H  ~+~ { }_{1}^{1} H \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }_{1}^{2} H  ~+~ { }_{+1}^{\,0} \beta ~+~ \nu &Q&=0.42 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
& Step 2:\quad & { }_{1}^{2} \mathrm{H} ~+~ { }_{1}^{1} \mathrm{H} \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He}  &Q&=5.48 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
& Step 3: \qquad & { }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ { }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He} \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ { }_{1}^{1} \mathrm{H} ~+~ { }_{1}^{1} \mathrm{H} &\quad Q&=12.9 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$

- The cycle begins with two protons forming a deuteron (Step 1). For this fusion, a proton needs to become a neutron, therefore the positron and the neutrino will be formed 

- Because this reaction is mediated by the weak force, it takes some time 

- The formed deuteron combines with another proton (Step 2), and forms helium-3, which, with another helium-3 reacts to form helium-4 (Step 3)

- Note, two of the steps $1 \;\&\; 2$ are needed for one step $3$ to take place

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Proton–proton cycle)

\seminormal

{7.5cm}
	
	- In total four protons turn into one He-4 (plus some extra positrons and neutrinos)

- Also $24.7 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ of energy is released:
$$\def\arraystretch{1.15}
{crr} 
2 \times \text { step } 1 & \qquad & 2 \times 0.42 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ 
+2 \times \text { step } 2 & \qquad & +2 \times 5.48 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ 
+1 \times \text { step } 3  & \qquad & +1 \times 12.9 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ 
\hline
&  & =\phantom{+2\;} 24.7 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$

- In total:
\[
4 \cdot { }_{1}^{1} H \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ 2 \beta^{+} ~+~ 2 \mathrm{\nu} ~+~ 24.7 \mathrm{\;MeV}
\]

- This proton–proton cycle is the primary energy source in our Sun. It is estimated that the reaction is occurring at the rate of $5.64 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{\;kg}$ of hydrogen per second fusing into helium producing $3.7 \times 10^{25} \;W$

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_31.png]

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Carbon cycle)

\semismall

	- Proton–proton cycle is assumed to be the dominating reaction occurring in our Sun, but in other stars of higher temperature and higher helium concentration, two helium-4 nuclei can form beryllium-8 
	\[
	 { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He} ~+~ { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He} \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{4}^{8} \mathrm{Be} 
	 \]

- ${ }_{4}^{8} Be$ is unstable and decays back to two heliums (in ${\sim} 10^{-16} \mathrm{\;s} $), but if another ${ }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}$ fusions with Be before its decay, a tightly bound ${ }_{\;6}^{12} C$ can be formed:

{7.7cm}

	- \[
{ }_{4}^{8} \mathrm{Be}  ~+~ { }_{2}^{4}\mathrm{He} \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{\;6}^{12} C
\]

- This C nucleus can begin the carbon cycle, which is a rapid way to "burn" hydrogen

- Note, that the carbon nucleus merely acts as a catalyst in this reaction scheme. Note also that there are heavier nuclei such as ${ }_{\;8}^{15} \mathrm{O}$ and ${ }^{15} N$ in the cycle, but they decay normally

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Carbon cycle)

	- Note, that as the outcome of the cycle, four protons produce one helium-4 and two positrons and two neutrinos 
	
	- But this reaction is much faster than the proton–proton cycle, because at no point does it require a proton to be converted into a neutron before the fusion reaction can happen

- The energy produced by one cycle is $26.7 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ i.e. almost equal to the energy released in the proton–proton cycle:
\[
4 \cdot { }_{1}^{1} \mathrm{H} \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ 2 \mathrm{e}^{+} ~+~ 2 \nu
\]

- In our Sun, proton–proton cycle dominates 

- In older and hotter stars, carbon cycle as well as also other types of fusion reactions are known to occur

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Nucleosynthesis: Origin of elements)

	- In the universe, hydrogen is most abundant element, then helium 
	
	- After the sudden drop, corresponding to lithium, beryllium and boron, the abundance follows a regularly decreasing trend 
	
	- The same pattern covers not only samples taken from various depths from Earth's crust, but also from the meteorites
	
- The abundance more or less level off at around $Z=35$ (or $A=80)$, but still it does have some pronounced maxima

- As for isotopic composition, lighter elements are richer with the stable isotopes with lower neutron content, while the heavier nuclei are richer in isotopes with higher neutron content 

- Interestingly, (stable) isotopes with $A=5$ and $A=8$ are missing from nature

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Nucleosynthesis: Origin of elements)

\semismall

	- It seems that, at least in our galaxy, the elements were all formed by approximately the same process
	
	- At an early time in the universe, there was a very large mass of gaseous hydrogen. Due to statistical fluctuations and gravitational interaction, some hydrogen condensed into clusters, and stars with high density $10^{5} \mathrm{\;kg} / \mathrm{m}^{3}$
	
	- Temperatures in stars/clusters increased and enabled proton–proton cycle, After that the helium-4 lead the reactions to larger nuclei 
	
	- Note, ${ }_{4}^{8} \mathrm{Be}$ is not stable, but fusion with ${ }_{1}^{2} \mathrm{H}$ or ${ }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He}$ may lead to ${ }_{\;5}^{10} B$, ${ }_{\;6}^{11} C$ etc.
	
	- The schematic presentation of a reaction where the beryllium captures a helium-4 and leads to so called 'helium burning' process:
	$$

&{ }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He} \quad &\rightarrow& \qquad { }_{4}^{8}\mathrm{Be} \qquad &(-0.0918 \mathrm{\;MeV}) \\
&{ }_{4}^{8} \mathrm{Be} ~+~ { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He} \quad &\rightarrow& \quad { }_{\;6}^{12} \mathrm{C} ~+~ 2 \gamma \qquad &(+7.367 \mathrm{\;MeV})

$$

- Ordinary fusion reactions reach to $A \approx 60$. Beyond that, neutron capture followed by $\beta^{-}$ decay leads to higher $Z$ (supernovas, merging neutron stars, etc.)

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Inducing the fusion reaction)

	- Fusion cannot occur spontaneously. In order to surpass the Coulombic repulsion (nuclei are positively charged), nuclei need to have large kinetic energies (= high temperature) 

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_32.png]

{7.5cm}

	- Once the nuclei are close to each other, within the range of the strong force, the nuclei can then "fall into the potential pit" (see Fig.)

- It is obvious that fusion requires high density and high temperatures 

- Once reactions start happening, large amount of energy will be released, as can be deduced form the steep left edge of the binding energy curve

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Fusion reactors)

	- A fusion reaction, which has a large cross section and which liberates a great amount of energy, is the reaction $D+T$, i.e. between deuterium $(D)$ and tritium $(T)$ 
	
	- However, since tritium is not readily available, the fusion of two deuterium nuclei $(D+D)$ is of more practical importance 
	
	- Also, it has the advantage that only one class of nuclei ($D$) is used 
	
	- Furthermore, $D+D$ fusion produces also tritium needed for the second reaction:
	$$
{ }_{\,1}^{2} \mathrm{D}  ~+~ { }_{\,1}^{2} \mathrm{D} \quad \rightarrow \quad 
{ {ll} { }^{3} \mathrm{T} ~+~ { }_{1}^{1} \mathrm{H} & \qquad Q=4.0 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ { }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ { }_{0}^{1} \mathrm{n} & \qquad Q=3.3 \mathrm{\;MeV} 

$$
and 
\[
{ }_{\,1}^{2} D  ~+~ { }_{\,1}^{3} \mathrm{T} \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ { }_{0}^{1} \mathrm{n} \qquad Q=17.6 \mathrm{\;MeV}
\]

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (Fusion reactors)

\semismall

{8.5cm}

	- One needs high energy for fusion. 
	
	The calculated temperature required to bring two deuterons within a distance of ${\sim} 2 \mathrm{\;fm}$ from each other is $T \sim 6 \times 10{ }^{9} \mathrm{\;K}$ 
	
	- There is, naturally, a velocity distribution, so some nuclei have a higher velocity than the average. Also tunneling helps. All in all,  temperatures of $10^{7}–10^{8} \;K$ may be sufficient to have the reaction occurring (as is the case in the Sun)

- In the real fusion reactors, the challenge is to confine the plasma exhibiting a sufficient temperature for the reactions to happen

- Two promising techniques are available:\\
Magnetic confinement $\rightarrow$ toroidal magnetic field\\
Inertial confinement $\rightarrow$ synchronized blows of the sample pellet from all sides, via laser beams illuminating the pellet

 (Comparison between fission and fusion)

	- 
	- Both techniques, fission and fusion, are based on strong force
- Only a small amount of fuel is needed (compared to fossil fuels)
- No waste $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ emitted $\rightarrow$ atmospheric sustainability
- Reaction products are radioactive $\rightarrow$ other environmental disadvantages
($\rightarrow$ final disposal??)

- Fission:

	- Fuels: uranium and thorium, need for expensive mining processes
- Process products: radioactive waste with long half-life (e.g. $T_{1/2}(\mathrm{Pu-239}) =24\;100$ years)

- Fusion:

	- Fuel: deuterium can be distilled from water, is abundant
- Process products: tritium and isotopes of helium, tritium is radioactive, but its half-life is short (12.3 years)

--- slide: Nuclear Reactions ---

 (On the energy yields)

\seminormal

	- Energy released in a single fusion reaction is much less than that released in fission. But normalized against the mass, for the deuteron–deuteron fusion reaction the energy is about $2 \times 10^{14} \;J$ per kg of fuel
	\implies{ Over double the value for uranium fission }

- Because deuterium is abundant (about 1 D per 7000 hydrogen atoms), and the cost of extracting D from water is low (a fraction of €/gram), fusion is considered to be the 'promising power source of the future'

- However, as has been empirically seen (e.g. ITER-reactors), it is still technically very challenging 

- Note also the timeline and what has been stated about fusion power:

	- "A promising power source to be available within 20 years..."\\
	 – statement from year $1985 !$

	  – statement in year $2020 !$

 (NMR)

	- Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR):
	
	- Just as for Zeeman effect for electrons in atoms, the energy levels for nuclei also split in external magnetic field

- The magnetic moment for a nucleus is 
\[
\vec{\mu}  = \gamma \vec{I} \,, \qquad \mu_{N}  = \frac{e \hbar}{2 m_{p}} \,, \qquad where \qquad |\, \vec{I} \,|  =  I(I+1)^{1 / 2} \hbar
\]

- The potential energy for the magnetic moment in an external magnetic field is 
\[
U  = -\vec{\mu} \cdot \vec{B}
\]

- The energy difference between up and down states then becomes
\[
\Delta E  = 2 ( \mu_{z} )_{\text {proton}} B
\]

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (NMR)

\seminormal 

	- When hydrogen atoms (e.g. protons in water) are irradiated with electromagnetic radiation, i.e. photons of $h f=\Delta E$, then we will have a resonance absorption, and subsequent emission

- In a magnetic field of $1 \mathrm{\;T}$, the energy difference $\Delta E=1.76 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{\;eV}$ corresponds to resonance frequency $f=42.5$ $\mathrm{\;MHz}$  (a radio frequency)

{7.5cm}

	- NMR is applied to probe internal magnetic structure of materials, and especially in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of living tissue, with negligible heating effect

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_17.png]

- NMR is best known due to MRI, but it is widely applied also in other scientific areas, such as observing the structures of complex molecules in chemistry

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (NAA)

	- Neutron Activation Analysis (NAA):
	- A sample containing ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ is exposed to a beam of slow neutrons

- Nuclide ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$ can be generated via reaction ${ }_{Z}^{A} M (n, \gamma)^{A+1} M$, but it is radioactive, thus it will emit $\beta$ particles or $\gamma$ rays

- Monitoring the emitted radiation, ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z} M$ can be identified, and furthermore concentration of ${ }_{Z}^{A} M $ in the sample can be determined

- This neatly enables chemical analysis, even different isotopes are distinguished from each other, without breaking the sample

- The neutron activation analysis (NAA) is suitable for analysing very small amounts of material, and often it is used 'when other physicochemical methods are not sensitive enough'

- Drawback is the difficult disposal of radioactive samples

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (NAA)

\seminormal

	- After exposure to thermal neutrons the activity of a nuclide follows:
\[
R(t)  = \lambda N(t)  = R_{0} (1-\rme^{-\lambda t} )
\]
where $\lambda=$ decay constant characteristic for ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$, and $R_{0}$ is the constant production rate of that isotope ($g$ in earlier Lecture)

- In the equation above, $R(t)$ is measured and $R_{0}$ is in principle computed from known values of intensity $I$ (specific for the set-up) and cross section (specific for the reaction leading to nuclei $ { }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$). $I$ is the neutron flux (given e.g. in neutrons/s$\mathrm{m}^{2}$)

- In general the rate for collisions/production of is ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$:
\[
R_{0}  = N_{0} \sigma I \qquad , \qquad [\sigma I]  = m^{2} \frac{\text {neutrons}}{s \cdot m^{2}}  = \frac{\text {neutrons}}{s}
\]
where $N_{0}$ is the number of ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ nuclei in the sample

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (NAA)

	- Thereby, we obtain $R(t)  = \lambda N(t)  = N_{0} \sigma I (1-\rme^{-\lambda t} )$ and if the half-life of the nuclide is short, we obtain a saturation $R(t)=R(t=\infty)=N_{0} \sigma I$

- Table shows saturation activities $R(\infty)$ (per mg of sample) for a few isotopes

- Number of ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ atoms in the sample is
\[
N_{0}  = \fraca{R(t=\infty)}{\sigma I} \,,
\]
and mass of ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ in sample is

{7.8cm}

	- 
	\[
 m ({ }_{Z}^{A} M)  = \frac{N_{0}}{N_{A}} M({ }_{Z}^{A} M)  = \frac{R(t=\infty)}{N_{A} \sigma I} M({ }_{Z}^{A} M) \,,
\]
- where $M({ }_{Z}^{A} M)$ is the atomic weight of the element and $N_{A}$ is Avogadro's number	

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (Dating)

	- Radioactive Dating:

\semismall

	- Nature contains radioactive isotopes:

	- Three heavy element $\alpha$-emitter chains (thorium, uranium, actinium)
- Isolated long-lived isotopes, such as ${ }^{40} \mathrm{K}$ with $T_{1 / 2}=1.25 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{\;y}$
- Isolated radioactive isotopes are activated by cosmic rays

- For geological aging ${ }^{40} \mathrm{K}$ and ${ }^{232} \mathrm{Th} ~(T_{1 / 2}=1.24 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{\;y})$ are used (for "old" rocks, or "younger" rocks)

- Present abundance ratio of two isotopes is measured, one of them being radioactive or a stable product of a radioactive decay, (so that its abundance is

{5.7cm}

	- time dependent), and compared against a previous abundance ratio, that is known to have existed when the material was formed

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (Dating)

\seminormal

	- In archeology, ${ }^{14} \mathrm{C}$-dating is an important tool 
	- ${ }^{14} \mathrm{C}$ is a $\beta^{-}$ emitter with half-life $T_{1 / 2}=5730$ years

- Due to the carbon cycle in nature, ratio of ${ }^{14} \mathrm{C} /{ }^{12} \mathrm{C}$ in living organisms equals the value in atmosphere (presently the ratio ${ }^{14} \mathrm{C} /{ }^{12} \mathrm{C}=1.35 \times 10^{-12})$ 
- When organism dies, compounds such as atmospheric $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ or hydrocarbons stop circulating through the organism 
\implies{ Carbon stops being renewed, and the radioactive ${ }^{14} \mathrm{C}$ starts to decay}

- Activity (decay rate) of one gram of carbon in the material allows to calculate the time of death of the organism. In reality ${ }^{14} \mathrm{C} /{ }^{12} \mathrm{C}$ fraction has been varying through years, which weakens the accuracy of dating process 

 \small
	- ${\sim}$9000 years ago the ratio was $1.5$ times the ratio of the present time
	- Nuclear experiments have increased the ratio
	- Use of fossil fuel has decreased the ratio (signs of the Anthropocene?)

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (Dating)

	- Let's find out how the age of the Earth can be estimated!

- In radioactive decay, where a parent $(P)$ nuclide decays into daughter $(D)$, the numbers of parent and daughter nuclei are respectively given by: 
\[
N_{P}(t)  = N_{0} \rme^{-\lambda t} \qquad , \qquad N_{D}(t)  = N_{0}-N_{P}
\]

- Then, at each given moment of time $t$, we have 
\[
t = \frac{1}{\lambda} \ln ( \frac{N_{0}}{N_{P}} \;)  = \frac{T_{1 / 2}}{\ln 2} \ln ( \frac{N_{0}}{N_{P}} \,)  = \frac{T_{1 / 2}}{\ln 2} \ln ( 1 + \frac{N_{D}}{N_{P}} \,)
\]

- Some isotope ratios (${ }^{238} \mathrm{U} /{ }^{206} \mathrm{Pb},~{ }^{87} \mathrm{Rb} /{ }^{87} \mathrm{Sr},~{ }^{40} \mathrm{K} /{ }^{40} \mathrm{Ar}$) are used as 'rock-clocks', e.g. indicating that the age of Earth's surface rock is around $4.5 \times 10^{9}$ years

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

 (Alarms)

	- Am-241 is an isotope belonging to Neptunium series 
	
	- Am-241 is used in conventional household fire alarms to ionize the air

- In clean air, the ions in air move easily to electrodes and create a small trickle current 
\implies{ this trickle current is continuously measured }

- If smoke particles enter the channel, they remove the ions (of the ionized gas molecules) from the air, electrical conductivity of the air decreases 
\implies{ the trickle current stops }

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

	- Particle-Induced X-ray and Gamma-ray Emission (PIXE and PIGE)
- PIXE: 
- 
	 - An elemental analysis of trace element concentrations of samples (for elements $Z>20$) 
	 - Sample is bombarded with low energy ions (few MeV), X-rays will be emitted from K- and L-shell electrons of the atoms
	 
	 - This offers a method to identify elements in the sample 
	 
	 - Depending on the ions used, the method can be very surface-sensitive 
	 
	 - PIXE can be used e.g. to detect elemental composition in trees (which have the annual growth rings) or e.g. verify genuinity of old paintings (with distinct elemental composition)

--- slide: Applications of Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity ---

	- Particle-Induced X-ray and Gamma-ray Emission (PIXE and PIGE)

	- PIGE: 
	- 
	 - Bombarded with higher ion energies, nuclei start emitting $\gamma$ rays from the nucleus in a similar way to PIXE
	 - Then also lighter elements can be detected 
	  
	 - For example, the ${ }^{23} \mathrm{Na}( \mathrm{p}, \mathrm{p}^{\prime} + \gamma ) {}^{23} \mathrm{Na}$ reaction is frequently used for determining bulk sodium content of glasses 

-
- PIXE and PIGE are both used widely in elemental analysis in material and environmental sciences

 (Ytimen koko)

- Verrannollisuukerroin ($r \propto A^{1/3}$) implikoi että  ytimen tilavuus on
\[
V_{t o t}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^{3}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi (  R_{0} A^{1 / 3} )  ^{3}  = \frac{4}{3} \pi R_{0}^{3} \times A
\]
- Neutroneilla ja protoneilla on lähestulkoon samat massat ($m_{p} \approx m_{n} \approx m_{nukleoni}$), jolloin ytimen kokonaismassa on verrannollinen $A$\,:\,han, implikoiden että kaikki ytimet ovat lähes yhtä tiheitä:
\[
\rho  = \fraca{\text {massa}}{\text {tilavuus}}  = \fraca{A \times m_{\text {nukleoni}}}{A \times \frac{4}{3} \pi R_{0}^{3}} \approx 2,3 \times 10^{17} \mathrm{\;kg} / \mathrm{m}^{3}
\]

- Vakioarvoinen tiheys implikoi että nukleonit ovat kokoonpuristumattomia ja tiiviisti pakkautuneita, eli että nukleoneilla on voimakkaasti hylkivät kovat ytimet

- Yksittäisen nukleonin efektiivinen säde  on noin $ R_{0} \sim 1 \mathrm{\;fm}$

 (Lyhyt yhteenveto)

\seminormal

	- Faktat:
-<.->[] 
	 - Ytimen nukleonitiheys on ${\sim}$vakioarvoinen, eikä riipu alkuaineesta 
	 - Ytimen varaustiheys on ${\sim}$vakio, eikä riipu alkuaineesta

- Yhteenveto:
Jos tiheys on vakioarvoinen, nukleonin lisääminen $(A \rightarrow A+1)$ ei kasvata tiheyttä. Eli jos on olemassa attraktiivinen voima, sen luonne poikkeaa sähköisestä vuorovaikutuksesta, mikä kasvaisi tekijällä $A$ mikäli yksi sähkövaraus lisättäisiin ytimeen. Vahvan vuorovaikutuksen täytyy siis olla lyhyen kantaman voima (noin nukleonin etäisyyden luokkaa)

- Ytimissä vaikuttavat voimat ovat erittäin vahvoja verrattuna sähköisiin voimiin (vedyn ionisaatioenergia on $13,6 \mathrm{\;eV}$, mutta tarvitaan ${\sim}8 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ irrottamaan nukleoni ytimestä)

- Attraktiivinen vuorovaikutusenergia on lähestulkoon yhtäsuuri protoneille ja neutroneille. Vahva vuorovaikutus ei siis riipu sähköisestä varauksesta

 (Malli vakaudelle/stabiilisuudelle)

	- Kaksinukleoninen ydin:\\
	Tarkastellaan kahta vahvan voiman toisiinsa sitomaa nukleonia kapeassa mutta syvässä potentiaalikaivossa (ks.\ kuva 4)

- 
	 - Kaksi protonia (p-p): Nukleonienvälinen vetovoima + sähköstaattinen repulsio $\rightarrow$ luultavasti epävakain vaihtoehto

- Kaksi neutronia (n-n): Vain nukleonienvälinen vahva vuorovaikutus
- Protoni–neutroni-pari (p-n): Vain nukleonienvälinen vahva vuorovaikutus $\rightarrow$ deuteroni

- Lopulta vain p-n (deuteroni) kombinaatio muodostaa vakaan ytimen. Miksi? Vahva vuorovaikutus riippuu spinistä, ja on vahvempi kun spinit ovat samansuuntaiset. Lisäksi kieltosääntö vaikuttaa kaikkien ytimien muodostumiseen. Protonit ja  neutronit ovat 1/2-spinin fermioneita, eivätkä voi olla samassa kvanttitilassa

--- slide: Ytimen Koossapysyminen ---

	- Kahden nukleonin tapauksessa matalimman energian tila on perustila jossa spinit ovat samansuuntaiset (suurempi vahva vuorovaikutus). Paulin kieltosääntö kieltää tällaiset tilat kombinaatioille n-n ja p-p

	- Koska ytimen tiheys oli pikemminkin radiaalinen kuin täysin homogeeninen, ja koska kaikkien ytimien tiheys on likimain vakio, on luonnollista kuvata ydintä nestepisaramallin avulla

- Analogia nestepisaraan toimii suhteellisen hyvin: 
- 
	- Neste koostuu tiiviisti pakkautuneista, järjestäytymättömistä molekyyleistä, jotka vetävät toisiaan puoleensa suuremmilla etäisyyksillä 
	- Pienemmillä etäisyyksillä ne hylkivät toisiaan, johtaen puristumattomuuteen, kuten esim.\ veden tapauksessa

- Jotta nestemolekyyli voidaan siirtää pisaran pinnalle, molekyyli täytyy vetää erilleen muista sitä puoleensavetävistä molekyyleistä. Tämä vaatii energiaa, jolloin pallomainen muoto johtaa matalimman energian tilaan, ts.\ minimoi pinta-alan

 (Nestepisaramalli)

\semismall

	- Pisaran sisällä olevia hiukkaset vetävät puoleensa kaikkia läheisyydessä olevia hiukkasia

- Jos kaikki hiukkaset olisivat pisaran sisällä, niin sidosenergia olisi suoraan verrannollinen hiukkasten lukumäärään.

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop.pdf]

 Pinnalla olevilla hiukkasilla on kuitenkin vähemmän naapureita, eli vähemmän sidoksia toisten hiukkasten kanssa \implies{ Sidosenergia pienenee }

{8.5cm}

	- Muodostetaan seuraavaksi yleinen kaava nestepisaran tavoin käyttäytyvän ytimen sidosenergialle 

- Yhteensä löytyy viisi vaikuttavaa tekijää (eli viisi termiä) jotka vaikuttavat sidosenergian suuruuteen

- Määritellään vakiot $c_{1}, c_{2}, c_{3}, c_{4}$, ja $c_{5}$ sovittamalla kokeellinen data muodostettuun malliin [jonka on oltava $f(A,N,Z)$]

- Tavoitteena on rakentaa semi-empiirinen kaava tunnettujen ytimien sidosenergioille

 (Tilavuustermi)

	- Kokonaan pisaran sisällä olevat hiukkaset ovat täysin naapureidensa ympäröimiä.  Jos kaikki hiukkaset olisivat pisaran sisällä, niin sidosenergia olisi suoraan verrannollinen hiukkasten (eli ytimen nukleonien) lukumäärään jotka muodostavat pisaran.
\[
BE \propto c_{1} A
\]

{8.5cm}

- Verrannollisuuskerroin $c_{1}$ voitaisiin teoreet\-ti\-sesti laskea, mutta on helpompaa vain muodostaa malli ja lopuksi sovittaa  vakiot kokeelliseen dataa

	- Eli tilavuustermi on muotoa  \[
c_{1} A
\]

 (Tilavuustermi)

\semismall

	- K: Onko sovitus/verrannollisuukertoimiin $c_{i}$ pohjautuva empiirinen malli hyödyllinen?
- V: Malli kuvaa monimutkaista systeemiä, ja se perustuu mittaustuloksiin

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_14.png]

- Sidososuudet muodostavat monimutkaisen funktion $\mathrm{N}$:n ja $\mathrm{Z}$:n suhteen, sen matemaattista muotoa ei voida eksplisiittisesti nähdä

	- Yksinkertaisen $Z$:sta ja $N$:sta riippuvan polynomisovitteen
\[
c_{1} Z^{2}  + c_{2} N  + c_{3} 
\]

{6.5cm}

- avulla ei saavuteta hyvää ennus\-tet\-ta\-vuutta, tai saada hyvää fysikaalista in\-tui\-tio\-ta ytimen käyttäytymiseen

- Koska nestepisaramalli sopii niin hyvin kokeellisiin tuloksiin, on järkevää olettaa että malli ja sen alkuoletukset ovat toimivia

- Mallin avulla voidaan myös ennustaa tuntemattomien nuklidien sidosenergioita (ennustettavuus)

 (Pintatermi)

	- Pinnan nukleoneilla on vähemmän puoleensavetäviä naapureita kuin sisäosan nukleoneilla. Joten pinnalta pitäisi olla helpompaa irrottaa nukleoni \implies{ Pinnan nukleonien sidosenergia on alhaisempi kuin sisänukleonien }

- Muistellaan yhtälöä
$ \quad 
V  = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^{3} \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = ( \frac{3}{4 \pi} V )^{1 / 3}
$
- Pinta-alaksi saadaan $=4 \pi r^{2} =4 \pi ( \frac{3}{4 \pi} V)^{2 / 3} \propto$\;V$^{2 / 3}$

{8.5cm}

- Sidosenergian pitäisi siis pienentyä pinta-alaan verrannollisella tekijällä, mikä on verrannollinen tilavuuteen$^{2 / 3}$

	- Koska tilavuus on verrannollinen nukleonien lukumäärään, saadaan verrannollisuus:
\[
BE \propto  - c_{2} A^{2 / 3}
\]

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop_surface.pdf]

--- slide: Ydinmallit ---

 (Coulombinen termi)

	- Coulombinen (sähköinen) vuorovaikutus protonien välillä nostaa potentiaalienergiaa  $\Rightarrow$ Coulombinen vuorovaikutus pienentää sidosenergiaa 

- Ytimessä on $Z$ protonia, jotka hylkivät toisia $Z-1$ protonia

- Coulombinen energiatermi, joka on verrannollinen termiin $\frac{e^{2}}{r}$, vaikuttaa siis kuten alla:

{8.5cm}

	- 
	$$
	
\frac{Z(Z-1)}{<protonien etäisyys>} &\propto \frac{Z(Z-1)}{pisaran säde}\\ 
&\propto \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1 / 3}} \\
\Rightarrow \qquad  \qquad 
B E &\propto -c_{3} \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1 / 3}} 

$$

[FIGURE: Tikz_SOM_Nuclear_liquid_drop_coulomb.pdf]

 (Epäsymmetriatermi (Paulin termi))

{8cm}

	- Pienemmillä ytimillä $N \cong Z$ mutta isommilla $N>Z$.

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_13.png]

 Tämä vaikuttaa energiatasojen protonien ja neutronien väliseen miehitystasapainoon. Ilmiö on kvanttimekaaninen ja on seurausta kieltosäännöstä

- Jos neutroneita on enemmän, niin suurempi määrä energiatiloja tulee miehitetyksi (kaksi neutronia per taso) ja systeemien ko\-ko\-nais\-e\-nergia kasvaa

- Ja koska meillä on prosessi joka kasvattaa energiaa, nukleonien välinen sidosenergia pienentyy

--- slide: Ydinmallit ---

 (Epäsymmetriatermi (Paulin termi))

\small

	- Tarkastellaan tilannetta missä neutronit ja protonit ovat miehittäneet energiatilat yhtä korkealle, $E_{F}$, ja oletetaan energiatilat tasavälisiksi ($\delta E$)

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_15.png]

{8.75cm}

	- Jos $j$ neutronia muuttuu protoneiksi (tai päinvastoin), ylimpien neutroneiden täytyy siirtyä $E_{F}$:llä olevalta tilalta ylemmälle protonitilalle $E_{F}+(j / 2) \delta E$. Muiden täytyy tehdä vastaava hyppäys. Jos jokaisen hiukkasen energia kasvaa termillä $(j / 2) \delta E$, kokonaisenergia kasvaa määrällä $(j^{2} / 2) \delta E$

- Ilmaistaan nyt $j$ neutroni- ja protonilukujen avulla ($N$ ja $Z$). Ensin $N=Z$, sitten tapahtuu $j$:n protonin lisäys ja $j$:n neutronin vähennys. $Z-N$ täytyy nyt olla $2 j$, jolloin $j=\frac{1}{2}(Z-N)$. Eli epäsymmetrian $Z$:n ja $N$:n välillä pitäisi pienentää sidosenergiaa (kasvattaa kokonaisenergiaa) tekijällä
\[
(N-Z)^{2} \delta E  = (A-2 Z)^{2} \delta E \quad \Rightarrow \quad BE \propto -c_{4} \frac{(A-2 Z)^{2}}{A}
\]
missä $A$:lla jakaminen normalisoi termin

--- slide: Ydinmallit ---

 (Kytkeytymistermi (paritermi))

	- Mittausten perusteella on selvää että vahva voima suosii protonien ja neutronien kytkeytymistä. Tämä viimeinen termi$^{*}$ mahdollistaa paremman sovitteen tekemisen\footnoteleft{{$^*$Huomaa että tämä 5:s (kytkeytymis)termi puuttuu Harrisin kirjasta}}

- Termi ottaa huomioon sen tosiasian että nukleoni–nukleoni vuorovaikutus ei pelkästään nosta keskimääräistä nukleonien kokemaa potentiaalia, vaan että sillä on samanlaisia ominaisuuksia kuin suprajohteissa tapahtuvissa elektroniparien välisissä vuorovaikutuksissa (Cooperin parien vuorovaikutus)

- Tämä energiatermi on:
- 
	- Positiivinen (sitova) jos sekä $Z$ että $N$ ovat parillisia,
- negatiivinen (hajottava) jos sekä $Z$ että $N$ ovat parittomia, nolla muuten

 (Semi-empiirinen sidosenergian kaava)

B E = c_{1} A  - c_{2} A^{2 / 3}  - c_{3} \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1 / 3}}  - c_{4} \frac{(A-2 Z)^{2}}{A} \pm c_{5} A^{-4 / 3}
\]

&c_{4}=23,7 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\
&c_{5}=15,8 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$

\phantom{Oletetaan $A=100 \quad$}$\Rightarrow \quad r \approx 5,5 \mathrm{\;fm}$

- Nukleonin massa on $m_{p}=1,67 \times 10^{-27} \mathrm{\;kg} $

- $\Rightarrow$ Energia: $\quad E=\frac{\hbar^{2} \pi^{2}}{2 m_{p} L^{2}} n^{2}=3,3 \times 10^{-13} \;J \cdot n^{2} \cong(2 \mathrm{\;MeV}) \cdot n^{2}$

 (Gammahajoaminen)

	- Gammahajoamisessa

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_24.png]

 virittynyt ydin hajoaa alemmalle tilalle emittoiden fotonin, jolloin isotooppi ei muutu. Prosessi on analoginen virittyneen atomitilan relaksaatioprosessin kanssa, jossa myös emittoituu fotoni

 (Yhdistetty alfa/beeta- ja gammahajoaminen)

	- Kun ydin on emittoinut $\alpha$-, tai $\beta$-hiukkasia,  ydin jää yleensä virittyneeseen tilaan, ja emittoi myös $\gamma$-säteilyä 
	
	- Näiden virittyneiden tilojen elinajat ovat usein erittäin lyhyitä, paitsi silloin kun valintasäännöt kieltävät virittyneen tilan purkautumisen (vrt.\ fosforenssi)

- Yleisesti ottaen isotooppien $\gamma$-säteilyn energiatasot ovat aika karakteristisia, ja niitä voidaan käyttää sormenjälkinä, eli tunnistamaan tiettyjä isotooppeja, ja myös antamaan lisäinformaatiota esim. ydinten kuorien energiatasoista (ks.\ aiempi kuva torium-227:n emissiospektristä)

 (Sisäinen konversio)

	- Virittynyt ydin voi relaksoitua gammasäteilyn emittoinnin lisäksi myös sisäisen konversion kautta

- Konversiossa virittyneen ytimen energia siirtyy atomin elektronille, joka emittoituu atomista suurella nopeudella
 
 - Tällaista tapahtuu lähinnä $s$-elektroneille $K$- ja $L$-kuorilla, joilla on suurehko todennäköisyys sijaita lähellä atomin ydintä 
 
 - Energia on tällöin muotoa:
\[
hf  =  ( E_{\text {korkea}}-E_{\text {matala}} ) -E_{\text {elektroni}}
\]

- Sisäinen konversio on yhden vaiheen mutta usean hiukkasen prosessi, vaikka periaatteessa sen voidaan ajatella tapahtuvan kahdessa vaiheessa, emittoiden ja absorboiden $\gamma$-fotonin

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

	- Yksittäinen radioaktiivinen ydin hajoaa vain kerran. Prosessi on luonteeltaan tilastollinen, jolloin tiedetään vain keskimääräinen aika jonka kuluessa hajoaminen tapahtuu

- Jos ytimien lukumäärä $N$ on tarpeeksi suuri, hajoamisprosessin tilastollisen luonteen takia ytimien hajoamista voidaan tarkastikin kuvata hajoamislailla 

- Hajonneiden ytimien lukumäärä, eli muutos alkuperäisten ytimien lukumäärässä, $d N$, on verrannollinen sekä aikaan että radioaktiivisten ytimien lukumäärään:
\[
\{}\! {l}
d N \propto d t \\
d N \propto N
 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad d N \propto N d t
\]
ja 
\[
\Rightarrow \qquad \frac{d N}{d t} \propto N \quad \text { eli } \quad \frac{d N}{d t} =  - \lambda N \quad \text { tai } \quad \frac{d N}{N}  =  - \lambda d t
\]

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Vaimenemisnopeus)

	- Muodostettu differentiaaliyhtälö voidaan ratkaista jolloin saadaan ajasta riippuva jäljellä olevien ydinten lukumäärä:
\[
\frac{d N}{N}  =  - \lambda d t \quad \Rightarrow \quad \int_{N_{0}}^{N} \frac{d N^{\prime}}{N^{\prime}}  =  - \lambda \int_{0}^{t} d t \quad \Rightarrow \quad \ln \frac{N}{N_{0}} =  - \lambda t \quad \Rightarrow \quad N(t)= N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}
\]
- hajoamisvakio $\lambda$ ($[\lambda]=\frac{1}{s}$) on jokaiselle ytimelle tyypillinen vakio

- Voidaan määrittää positiivinen suure $R=\lambda N(t)  =  |\! \frac{d N}{dt} \!| $, joka tunnetaan vaimenemisnopeutena

- Vaimenemisnopeus voidaan kirjoittaa myös muodossa 
\[
 R(t)= | \frac{d N}{d t} |  = \lambda N(t) = \lambda N_{0} e^{-\lambda t} = R_{0} e^{-\lambda t}
\]

- Huomaa että ydinten lukumäärä $N$ ja hajoamisvakio $R$ pienenevät eksponentiaalisesti ajan suhteen

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Puoliintumisnopeus)

\seminormal

	- Aika jonka jälkeen ytimistä on puolet jäljellä on puoliintumisaika $T_{1 / 2}$:

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_26.png]
[footnote: Figure from R. Harris, Modern Physics (2nd edition).]
\[
 N ( T_{1 / 2} )   =  N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1 / 2}}  = \frac{1}{2} N_{0} \qquad
\Rightarrow \qquad  - \lambda T_{1 / 2} = \ln \frac{1}{2} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1 / 2}}
\]
- Ytimiä sitovat prosessit ovat monimutkaisia, ja usein ytimillä on monia hajoamistapoja. On siis hankalaa ennustaa ytimen puoliintumisaikaa $T_{1 / 2}$. Taulukkoarvoja löytyy suurimmalle osalle isotoopeista, ja $T_{1 / 2}$ vaihtelee $10^{-22}\;s$ ja $10^{17}$ vuoden välillä

{6.5cm}

- Esimerkiksi hiili-14 puoliintumisaika (radiohiiliajoitus) on 5730 vuotta. amerikium-241:n (palohälyttimet) puo\-liin\-tu\-misaika $T_{1 / 2}$ on 432,2 vuotta.

- Esimerkki lyhytikäisestä isotoopista on fluori-18 (isotooppilääketiede). Sen puoliintumisaika on 109 min.  

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Aineen (radio)aktiivisuus)

	- Hajoamisnopeus $R(t)=\lambda N$ kertoo myös $N$:stä radioaktiivisesta ytimestä koostuvan kappaleen aktiivisuuden  (tai 'radioaktiivisuuden') 
\[
[ R ]  =  yksikköajassa hajoavien aktiivisten ydinten lukumäärä
\]

- Radioaktiivisuuden SI-yksikkö on becquerel (Bq), kun taas vanha yksikkö (yhä käytössä) on curie (Ci)
\[
1 \mathrm{\;Bq}  = 1 \text {\;hajoamista} / \mathrm{s} \quad , \quad 
1 \mathrm{\;Ci}  =  3,7 \times 10^{10} \text {\;hajoamista} / \mathrm{s}=3,7 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{\;Bq}
\]
- $1 \mathrm{\;Ci}$ vastaa $1 \mathrm{\;g}$ radiumin aktiivisuutta $({ }_{~88}^{226}Ra$,  $T_{1/2}=1600$ a)

- Aktiivisuuden $1 \mathrm{\;mCi}=37 \mathrm{\;MBq}$ omaava lähde on todella voimakas lähde (riippuen tosin vähän säteilyn tyypistä)

- ${ }^{241} \mathrm{Am}$:n aktiivisuus palohälyttimissä on tyypillisesti $1 \;\mu \mathrm{Ci}=37 \mathrm{\;kBq}$

 (Esimerkkilasku)

	- K: Säiliössä on $2 \;\mu$g tritiumia jonka puoliintumisaika on 12,3 vuotta ja atomimassa 3,02u.
(a) Mikä on sen alkuhajoamisnopeus ja aktiivisuus?
(b) Milloin lähteen hajoamisnopeus on pienentynyt $1 %$:in lähtöarvostaan?

- A: (a) Hajoamisvakio on $R(t)=\lambda N$, jolloin alkuhajoamisnopeus on:
\[
R_{0} = R(t=0) = \lambda N_{0} = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1 / 2}} \frac{m_{tot}}{m_{1}}  = \frac{\ln 2}{12,3 \;y} \frac{2 \times 10^{-9} \;kg}{3,02 \;u \cdot 1,66 \times 10^{-27} \;kg / u}  = 7,1 \times 10^{8} \;s^{-1}
\]
ja vastaava aktiivisuus  on $7,1 \times 10^{8} \mathrm{\;Bq}=710 \mathrm{\;MBq}$

- (b) Kysyttiin milloin $\lambda N(t)=\frac{1}{100} \lambda N_{0}$? 
$$

 &\Rightarrow \qquad& \lambda N_{0} e^{-\lambda t} & = \frac{1}{100} \lambda N_{0} 
\qquad \Rightarrow \qquad  - \lambda t = \ln \frac{1}{100} \\
 &\Rightarrow \qquad&  t& =  - \ln \frac{1}{100} / ( \frac{\ln 2}{12,3 \;a} \,) = 2,6 \times 10^{9} \;s=82 \;a

$$

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Hajoamissarjat)

	- Tarkastellaan radioaktiivisia hajoamissarjoja: $A \stackrel{\lambda_{A}}{\rightarrow} B \stackrel{\lambda_{B}}{\rightarrow} C$, missä hajoamisvakiot $A$:lle ja $B$:lle ovat $\lambda_{A}$ ja $\lambda_{B}$ 
	- Tavoitteena on löytää nuklidin $B$ ajastariippuva lukumäärä, $N_{B}=N_{B}(t)$, olettamalla että alun lukumäärät ($t=0)$ nuklideille $A$ ja $B$ ovat $N_{A0}$ ja $N_{B0}$, vastaavasti

- Aine A hajoaa nopeudella $\frac{d N_{A}}{d t} =-\lambda_{A} N_{A}$, johtaen ehtoon $N_{A}(t)=N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}$

- Ainetta $B$ muodostuu A:n hajotessa, mikä myös itsessään hajoaa:
\[
\frac{d N_{B}}{d t}  =  + \lambda_{A} N_{A} - \lambda_{B} N_{B} =  + \lambda_{A} N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} - \lambda_{B} N_{B}
\]

- Tämä on differentiaaliyhtälö $N_{B}$:lle. Ratkaistaksemme $N_{B}=N_{B}(t)$, 'arvataan' yritefunktioksi yhtälö: 
\[
N_{B}(t)  = N_{A 0} (c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} )
\]

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Hajoamissarjat)

	- Sijoitetaan yrite saatuun differentiaaliyhtälöön ja derivoidaan:
$$

& & \frac{d N_{B}}{d t} = N_{A 0} (-\lambda_{A} c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}  - \lambda_{B} c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} ) & =  + \lambda_{A} N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} - \lambda_{B} N_{A 0} (c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} ) \qquad \quad \\
&\Rightarrow&  - \lambda_{A} c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}-\lambda_{B} c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t}& =  + \lambda_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} - \lambda_{B} (c_{A} e^{-\lambda_{A} t}+c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} ) \\
&\Rightarrow& (\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{B} c_{A}+\lambda_{A} c_{A} ) e^{-\lambda_{A} t}& = 0 \\
&\Rightarrow& \lambda_{A}-\lambda_{B} c_{A}+\lambda_{A} c_{A}&=0 \\
&\Rightarrow& c_{A}& = \fraca{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}

$$
Eli saadaan yhtälö 
\[
N_{B}(t)  = N_{A 0} ( \frac{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} t} )
\]
- missä tekijä $c_{B}$ on vielä tuntematon

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Hajoamissarjat)

	- Jos alkuarvo $N_{B}$:lle olisi 0 ajanhetkellä $t=0$, ts.
\[
N_{B}(t=0)  = N_{A 0} ( \frac{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}} e^{-\lambda_{A} \cdot 0}  + c_{B} e^{-\lambda_{B} \cdot 0} ) =0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad c_{B}=\frac{-\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}
\]

- Joten ytimen B lukumääräksi $N_{B}(t)$ saadaan
\[
N_{B}(t)  = N_{A 0} \frac{\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}
( e^{-\lambda_{A} t}-e^{-\lambda_{B} t} \,)
\]

- Käytännössä aktiivisuus on usein käytetty suure

- Ytimien $A$ ja $B$ aktiivisuudet ovat
$$

R_A = \lambda_{A} N_{A} & = \lambda_{A} N_{A 0} e^{-\lambda_{A} t} \\
R_B = \lambda_{B} N_{B} & = N_{A 0} \frac{\lambda_{A} \lambda_{B}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}} ( e^{-\lambda_{A} t}-e^{-\lambda_{B} t} \,) 

$$

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Ydinten synnyttäminen)

	- Radioaktiivisia ytimiä syntyy neutronikaappauksen seurauksena (joskus myös elektroni- ja protonisäteitä käytetään) 
	
	- Esimerkiksi ${ }^{59}$Co:a pommitetaan neutroneilla synnytettäessä ${ }^{60}$Co:a mikä on radioaktiivinen ydin jonka puoliintumisaika on $5,27$ vuotta

- Oletetaan että radioaktiivisia ytimiä synnytetään vakionopeudella $g$ ydintä/s, ja että hajoamisvakio on $\lambda$, ja lasketaan synnytettyjen radioaktiivisten ydinten lukumäärä yksikköajassa:
$$

\frac{d N}{d t} & = g-\lambda N \\
\int_{N_{0}}^{N}  \frac{d N}{N-g / \lambda} & =  - \lambda \int_{0}^{t} d t \quad \Rightarrow  \quad \ln \fraca{N-g / \lambda}{N_{0}-g / \lambda}  =  - \lambda t

$$

--- slide: Radioaktiivinen Hajoamislaki ---

 (Ydinten synnyttäminen)

	- $$

& & \ln \frac{N-g / \lambda}{N_{0}-g / \lambda} & =  - \lambda t \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad N-g / \lambda& =  ( N_{0}-g / \lambda )  e^{-\lambda t} \\
&\Rightarrow \quad& N(t)& = \frac{g}{\lambda} ( 1-e^{-\lambda t} ) +N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}

$$

- Jos aluksi ei ole aktiivisia ytimiä, eli $N_{0}=0$, niin aktiivisten ydinten lukumääräksi saadaan
\[
N(t)  = \frac{g}{\lambda} ( 1-e^{-\lambda t} ) 
\]

- Huomaa että suurin tuotettu ydinten lukumäärä on $N_{\max }=\frac{g}{\lambda}$

- Lisäksi näytteen aktiivisuus on $\lambda N(t)=g ( 1-e^{-\lambda t} ) $, jonka maksimi on $g$. Huomaa, että esimerkki pätee suoraan neutroniaktivaatioanalyysiin (käsitellään myöhemmin)

 (Vuorovaikutusala ja peittoaste)

	- Tarkastellaan pienten hiukkasten jatkuvatoimista sädettä joka osuu kohdepinnan hiukkasiin. Yksinkertaistetaan tilannetta oletuksilla:

	- Pommittavat hiukkaset ovat pieniä, pistemäisiä
- Kohdehiukkasten vuorovaikutusala on $\sigma,~[\sigma]=m^{2}$
- Kohdehiukkasilla on  pieni lukumäärätiheys $n$ jolloin ne eivät mene toistensa päälle

- Yhteenlaskettu (projisoitu) kohdehiukkasten ala kohdepinnassa on:
\[
A^{\prime}  = \Underlinecomment{red}{n \cdot(A d x)}{\qquad Tilavuudessa Adx olevien kohdehiukkasten lukumäärä} \cdot \sigma
\]

- (kohdepinnan projisoidun alan) peittoaste on 
\[
\fraca{A^{\prime}}{A}  = \frac{n A d x \sigma}{A} = n \sigma d x
\]

 (Eksponentiaalinen vaimeneminen)

\seminormal

	- Jos jokainen osuva hiukkanen vuorovaikuttaa kerran, osuvien hiukkasten osuus (tulevista $N$:stä hiukkasesta) joka osuu paksuudeltaan $d x$ olevaan pintaan on
\[
\frac{d N}{N}  = n \sigma d x
\]
- mutta on käytännöllisempää tarkastella osuutta joka häviää tulevasta hiukkassäteestä:
\[
\frac{d N}{N}  =  - n \sigma d x
\]

- Nyt tarkastellaan hiukkaslukumäärää $N$ jotka osuvat $x$:n paksuiseen pintaan. Nyt saadaan tulevien hiukkasten lukumääräksi, jotka jäävät säteeseen etäisyyden $x$ funktiona:
\[
\int_{N_{0}}^{N} \fraca{d N}{N} = \int_{0}^{x} - n \sigma d x \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad N(x) = N_{0} e^{-n \sigma x}
\]
Eli olemme johtaneet eksponentiaalisen vaimenemisen lain

 (Luonnolliset ja keinotekoiset ydinreaktiot)

	- Ydinreaktiot voivat olla:

	- Luonnollisia: kuten aiemmin kuvatut radioaktiiviset reaktiot. Tällöin reaktion $Q$-arvon täytyy olla positiivinen
- Keinotekoinen: tuleva (energeettinen) hiukkanen törmää kohdeytimeen ja käynnistää reaktion

- Kun kohdeydintä pommitetaan (projektiili)hiukkasilla, törmäys voi olla:

	- Elastinen: Tulevat hiukkaset siroavat (elastisesti) kohdeytimen potentiaalikaivon reunasta $\rightarrow$ saapuvan hiukkasen liikemäärä ja sen suunta muuttuu
- Epäelastinen: suoran vuorovaikutuksen  seurauksena (yleensä ensiksi muodostuu väliaikainen 'väliydin') ydin hajoaa reaktiotuotteiksi

 (Epäelastinen sironta \& Keinotekoinen ydinreaktio)

	- Epäelastista sirontaa tapahtuu usein keskitason energioilla, alle ${\sim}140 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ 
	\implies{ Ylimääräisiä hiukkasia ei vielä muodostu (kuten mesoneita)}
	
	 - Kohdeytimen ($X$) törmäys  projektiilin ($x$) kanssa synnyttää väliytimen
	 
	 -  Väliydin hajoaa lopputuoteytimeksi ($Y$), usein reaktiotuotteina on lisäksi muita hiukkasia ($y$)
	 
	 - Reaktioyhtälö voidaan tällöin kirjoittaa muodossa 
\[
 x ~ + ~ X \quad \rightarrow \quad Y ~ + ~ y ~ + ~ Q  
\]
tai vähän ytimekkäämmin hyödyntäen sulkunotaatiolla:
\[

\]

\[
 X(x, y) Y
 \]

 (Törmäykset ja reaktion Q-arvo)

	- Millainen reaktio onkaan kyseessä, energian täytyy säilyä. Reaktiossa $X(x, y) Y$ vapautuvan energian määrää kutsutaan $Q$-arvoksi:
\[
x ~+~ X \quad \rightarrow \quad Y ~+~ y ~+~ Q  \;,  missä  \qquad Q=(m_{x}+m_{X}-m_{y}-m_{Y}) c^{2}
\]
- $Q>0$: eksoterminen reaktio (alkutuotteet ovat painavampia kuin lopputuotteet) 
- $Q<0$: endoterminen reaktio (alkutuotteet ovat kevyempiä)

- Esimerkkejä: 
$$ \hspace{2cm}

 \mathrm{n} ~ + ~ { }^{1} \mathrm{H} \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }^{2} \mathrm{H} ~ + ~ \gamma ~+~ 2,22 \mathrm{\;MeV} \quad  (Eksoterminen) \\
\gamma ~+~ { }^{2} \mathrm{H} \quad &\rightarrow \quad { }^{1} \mathrm{H} ~+~ \mathrm{n} ~-~ 2,22 \mathrm{\;MeV} \quad (Endoterminen)

$$

- Endotermisessä reaktiossa kynnysenergian täytyy ylittyä 

- Kynnysenergian arvo on $|Q|$. Mutta tämä pätee vain  massakeskipisteen (center-of-mass, CM) koordinaatistossa

 (Kinetiikka)

\seminormal

{9.2cm}

	- Tutkitaan reaktiota aluksi epärelativistisesti CM-koordinaatistossa, kun projektiili $x$ (massa $m$, nopeus $v_p$) osuu ytimeen $X$ (massa $M$, nopeus $V_y$):

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_50_cropped_a]
[footnote: \TiplerCRshort]

$$

\vec{p}_{m}+\vec{p}_{M}=0 \qquad &\Rightarrow \qquad |\vec{p}_{m} |= |\vec{p}_{M} | \equiv p \\
  \qquad &\Rightarrow \qquad m v_p=M V_y \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{m}{M}=\frac{V_y}{v_p}  \\
  E_{CM}  = \frac{p^{2}}{2 m}&  + \frac{p^{2}}{2 M} = \frac{p^{2}}{2}  ( \frac{m+M}{m M} \,)  \,, \qquad CM-koordinaatisto

$$

- Kun taas laboratoriokoordinaatistossa, 

[FIGURE: SOM_Tipler_Nuclear_physics_figure_50_cropped_b]

 projektiilin $\mathrm{x}$ nopeus on $v_p+V_y$, kohti levossa olevaa kohdetta $M$, jolloin liikemääräksi saadaan:
\[
p_{lab}  = m(v_p+V_y)  = m v_p ( 1+\frac{V_y}{v_p} \,)  = m v_p ( 1+\frac{m}{M} \,)  =  p ( 1+\frac{m}{M} \,) \hspace{3.5cm}
\] 
Ja energiaksi
\[
E_{lab}  = \frac{p_{lab}^{2}}{2 m}  = \frac{p^{2}}{2 m} ( \frac{m+M}{M} \,)^{2}  = \frac{m+M}{M} E_{C M} \hspace{3.5cm}
\]

\seminormal

- pp-ketju käynnistyy kahden protonin muodostaessa deuteriumin (Reaktio 1). Reaktiossa protoni muuttuu neutroniksi, synnyttäen myös positronin ja neutriinon 

- Tämän reaktion käynnistyminen edellyttää heikkoa vuorovaikutusta, jolloin prosessi vaatii aikaa 

- Muodostunut deuteriumi yhdistyy protonin kanssa (Reaktio 2) muodostaen helium-3:n, mikä fuusioituu toisen helium-3:n kanssa muodostaen helium-4:n (Reaktio 3)

- Huomaa että kaksi reaktiota $1 \;\&\; 2$ tarvitaan yhteen reaktio $3$:en

 (Protoni–protoni-ketju (pp-ketju))

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_31.png]

\seminormal

{7.5cm}
	
	- Yhteensä 4 protonia muuntuu He-4:ksi (syntyy myös positroneja ja neutrii\-noita)

- Myös $24,7 \mathrm{\;MeV}$ energiaa vapautuu:
$$\def\arraystretch{1.15}
{rrr} 
~2 \times \text { vaihe } 1 & \qquad & 2 \times 0,42 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ 
+~2 \times \text { vaihe } 2 & \qquad & +2 \times 5,48 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ 
+~1 \times \text { vaihe } 3  & \qquad & +1 \times 12,9 \mathrm{\;MeV} \\ 
\hline
&  & =\phantom{+2\;} 24,7 \mathrm{\;MeV}

$$

\[
4 \cdot { }_{1}^{1} H \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{2}^{4} \mathrm{He}  ~+~ 2 \beta^{+} ~+~ 2 \mathrm{\nu} ~+~ 24,7 \mathrm{\;MeV}
\]

- Tämä pp-ketju on aurinkomme pääenergianlähde. Reaktio polttaa arviolta nopeudella $5,64 \times 10^{11} \mathrm{\;kg}$ vetyä per sekunti, tuottaen samalla teholla $3,7 \times 10^{25} \;W$ energiaa

 (Hiilisykli)

\semismall

{ }_{4}^{8} \mathrm{Be}  ~+~ { }_{2}^{4}\mathrm{He} \quad \rightarrow \quad { }_{\;6}^{12} C

 (NMR)

	- Ydinmagneettinen resonanssi (NMR):
	
	- Kuten Zeemanin ilmiö atomin elektroneilla,  myös ytimen energiatasot jakaantuvat ulkoisessa magneettikentässä

- Ytimen magneettinen momentti on  
\[
\vec{\mu}  = \gamma \vec{I} \,, \qquad \mu_{N}  = \frac{e \hbar}{2 m_{p}} \,, \qquad missä \qquad |\, \vec{I} \,|  =  I(I+1)^{1 / 2} \hbar
\]

- Ulkoisessa magneettikentässä olevan magneettimomentin potentiaalienergia on
\[
U  = -\vec{\mu} \cdot \vec{B}
\]

- Energiaero jakaantuneiden tilojen välillä on
\[
\Delta E  = 2 ( \mu_{z} )_{\text {protoni}} B
\]

--- slide: Ydinfysiikan ja Radioaktiivisuuden Sovelluskohteet ---

 (NMR)

\seminormal 

	- Kun vetyatomeja (esim.\ veden protoneja) viritetään sähkömagneettisella säteilyllä, eli fotoneilla $h f=\Delta E$, tapahtuu resonanssiabsorptiota, ja vastaavaa emissiota

- $1 \mathrm{\;T}$ magneettikentässä, energiaero $\Delta E=1,76 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{\;eV}$ vastaa resonanssitaajuutta $f=42,5$ $\mathrm{\;MHz}$  (radiotaajuus)

{7.5cm}

	- NMR:ää sovelletaan materiaalien sisäisen magneettisen rakenteen tutkimiseen, ja erityisesti elävien kudosten magneettiresonanssikuvaukseen (MRI), minimoiden kudoksien lämpenemisen

[FIGURE: SOM_Nuclear_physics_Figure_17.png]

- NMR on parhaiten tunnettu MR-kuvauksesta, mutta sitä käytetään paljon myös tutkimuksessa, kuten mo- nimutkaisten kemiallisten molekyylien rakenteiden karakterisointiin

 (NAA)

	- Neutroniaktivaatioanalyysi (NAA):
	- ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ sisältävää näytettä pommitetaan hitailla neutroneilla 

- Nuklidi ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$ voi muodostua reaktiosta ${ }_{Z}^{A} M (n, \gamma)^{A+1} M$, mutta se on radioaktiivinen, eli se emittoi beetahiukkasia tai gammasäteitä

- Muodostuvaa säteilyä mittaamalla, ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z} M$ voidaan tunnistaa, ja lisäksi isotoopin ${ }_{Z}^{A} M $ pitoisuus näytteessä voidaan määrittää

- Tämä mahdollistaa näppärästi kemiallisen analyysin, jopa erilaisia isotooppeja voidaan erottaa toisistaan, hajottamatta näytettä

- Neutroniaktivaatioanalyysin (NAA) avulla voidaan analysoida pieniä määriä ainetta, ja sitä käytetään usein kun 'muut fysikokemiallisen menetelmät eivät ole tarpeeksi herkkiä'

- Menetelmän heikkoutena on radioaktiivisten näytteiden hävittäminen

--- slide: Ydinfysiikan ja Radioaktiivisuuden Sovelluskohteet ---

 (NAA)

\seminormal

	- Kun nuklideja on säteilytetty termisillä neutroneilla, niiden aktiivisuus noudattaa yhtälöä
\[
R(t)  = \lambda N(t)  = R_{0} (1-\rme^{-\lambda t} )
\]
missä $\lambda=$ hajoamisvakio joka riippuu ytimestä ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$, ja $R_{0}$ on isotoopin  muodostumisnopeus ($g$ aiemmissa luennoissa)

- Yhtälössä $R(t)$ mitataan ja $R_{0}$ voidaan periaatteessa laskea tunnettujen intensiteettien $I$ avulla (riippuu mittausjärjestelystä) ja vuorovaikutusalasta (riippuu reaktiosta joka johtaa nuklidiin $ { }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$). $I$ on neutronivuo (esim.\ yksiköissä neutronia/s$\cdot\mathrm{m}^{2}$)

- Yleisesti ottaen ytimen ${ }^{A+1}_{~\;Z}M$ törmäys/tuotantonopeus on:
\[
R_{0}  = N_{0} \sigma I \qquad , \qquad [\sigma I]  = m^{2} \frac{\text {neutronia}}{s \cdot m^{2}}  = \frac{\text {neutronia}}{s}
\]
missä $N_{0}$ on nuklidien ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ lukumäärä näytteessä

--- slide: Ydinfysiikan ja Radioaktiivisuuden Sovelluskohteet ---

 (NAA)

	- Näin saadaan $R(t)  = \lambda N(t)  = N_{0} \sigma I (1-\rme^{-\lambda t} )$ ja jos nuklidin puoliintumisaika on lyhyt, aktiivisuus saturoituu $R(t)=R(t=\infty)=N_{0} \sigma I$

- Taulukko näyttää saturaatioaktiivisuudet $R(\infty)$ (per näytemilligramma) muutamille isotoopeille

- Atomien ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$ lukumäärä näytteessä on
\[
N_{0}  = \fraca{R(t=\infty)}{\sigma I} \,,
\]
ja näytteessä olevan ${ }_{Z}^{A} M$:n massa on

{7.8cm}

	-<.->[] 
	\[
 m ({ }_{Z}^{A} M)  = \frac{N_{0}}{N_{A}} M({ }_{Z}^{A} M)  = \frac{R(t=\infty)}{N_{A} \sigma I} M({ }_{Z}^{A} M) \,,
\]
- missä $M({ }_{Z}^{A} M)$ on alkuaineen atomipaino ja $N_{A}$ on Avogadron luku
